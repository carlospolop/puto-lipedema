Lipedema tissue may be very painful and can severely impair mobility. Non-lipedema obesity, lymphedema, venous disease, and hypermobile joints are comorbidities. Lipedema tissue is difficult to reduce by diet, exercise, or bariatric surgery.</p></sec><sec id="sec2" disp-level="2"><title>Methods</title><p>This paper is a consensus guideline on lipedema written by a US committee following the Delphi Method. Consensus statements are rated for strength using the GRADE system.</p></sec><sec id="sec3" disp-level="2"><title>Results</title><p>Eighty-five consensus statements outline lipedema pathophysiology, and medical, surgical, vascular, and other therapeutic recommendations. Future research topics are suggested.</p></sec><sec id="sec4" disp-level="2"><title>Conclusion</title><p>These guidelines improve the understanding of the loose connective tissue disease, lipedema, to advance our understanding towards early diagnosis, treatments, and ultimately a cure for affected individuals.</p></sec><sec id="kwd-group1" sec-type="kwd-group" disp-level="2"><p><bold>Keywords:</bold> Lipedema, lymphedema, hypermobility, chronic venous disease, standard of care</p></sec></abstract><custom-meta-group><custom-meta><meta-name>status</meta-name><meta-value>released</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>display-pdf</meta-name><meta-value>yes</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-in-collection-domain</meta-name><meta-value>yes</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-olf</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-manuscript</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-preprint</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-journal-matter</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-scanned</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta><custom-meta><meta-name>is-retracted</meta-name><meta-value>no</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta><notes notes-type="article-notes"><sec id="historyarticle-meta1" sec-type="history" disp-level="2"><p>Issue date 2021 Dec.</p></sec></notes></front><body><sec id="sec1-02683555211015887" disp-level="1"><title>Introduction</title><p>Lipedema is a disease of fibrotic loose connective (adipose) tissue (LCT) on the lower abdomen, hips, buttocks, and limbs of females, sparing the trunk, hands, and feet. Lipedema is rare in men. A trigger for the development of lipedema tissue may be an increase in fluid and connective tissue remodeling that occurs alongside body changes during puberty, childbirth, menopause, stress associated with lifestyle change, or by altering tissue structure after surgery or trauma.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr1-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>
</sup> A hallmark of lipedema tissue is inflammation<sup><xref rid="bibr2-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="bibr3-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">3</xref></sup> resulting in tissue fibrosis and pain, and in some cases, the tissue may become numb.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr4-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>
</sup></p><p>First described in 1940 by Allen and Hines at Mayo Clinic in the US<sup>
<xref rid="bibr5-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>
</sup> and by Moncorps from Germany,<sup>
<xref rid="bibr6-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>
</sup> lipedema remains under-recognized in part, because it is assumed to be a usual hereditary component of female fat.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr7-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>
</sup> Lipedema is confused with non-lipedema obesity or lymphedema due to increased leg size.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr8-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>
</sup> Under-recognition or misdiagnosis can delay identification of lipedema for decades.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr7-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>
</sup> Therefore, patient access to appropriate and timely treatment is often diminished<sup>
<xref rid="bibr9-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>
</sup> and patients frequently find themselves blamed for their condition, including self-blame.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr10-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>
</sup> However, lipedema can be treated to reduce pain and edema, maintain mobility, and improve quality of life while slowing disease progression, therefore timely diagnosis is paramount.</p><p>Lipedema is identified by clinical exam<sup>
<xref rid="bibr11-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>
</sup> with diagnostic criteria to help guide the clinical diagnosis (<xref rid="fig1-02683555211015887" ref-type="fig">Figure 1</xref>). Skin and lipedema LCT are graded by stage and location (<xref rid="fig2-02683555211015887" ref-type="fig">Figure 2</xref>). Lipedema tissue, body mass index (BMI), metabolic disease and lymphedema increase with stage.<sup><xref rid="bibr4-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="bibr12-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">12</xref></sup></p><fig id="fig1-02683555211015887" position="float"><?disp-level 2?><label>Figure 1.</label><caption><p>Diagnostic considerations for lipedema supported by expert opinion of the United States standard of care committee. *∼30% of women with lipedema can have fat tissue on the hands likely due to loss of elasticity in the tissue.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr4-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>
</sup></p></caption><alternatives><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="image" xlink:href="10.1177_02683555211015887-fig1.jpg"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/c975/8652358/000990147da2/10.1177_02683555211015887-fig1.jpg?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status LOAD_COMPLETED?><?original-height 1388?><?original-width 3000?><?scaled-height 347?><?scaled-width 750?></graphic><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="thumb" xlink:href="10.1177_02683555211015887-fig1.gif"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/c975/8652358/3f6000da5061/10.1177_02683555211015887-fig1.gif?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?></graphic></alternatives></fig><fig id="fig2-02683555211015887" position="float"><?disp-level 2?><label>Figure 2.</label><caption><p>Stages and features of lipedema. A structured questionnaire of 96 consensus statements in REDCap<sup>
<xref rid="bibr18-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>
</sup> was completed by all panelists prior to the meeting, then panelist average responses were summarized, presented and discussed at the meeting (Round 1). After presentations on SOC guidelines from other countries including pathophysiology, diagnostic criteria,<sup><xref rid="bibr13-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>–<xref rid="bibr17-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">17</xref></sup> imaging, and medical, manual, and surgical treatments for patients with lipedema, consensus, defined as 75% agreement amongst panelists, was reached on 90 statements (Round 2). Panelist responses to the summary were collected, summarized again by representative panelists, and presented to panelists two more times to reach a final consensus on 85 statements following the Delphi survey technique.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr19-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>
</sup></p><p>Consensus statements were scored by panelists and averaged using the GRADE system<sup>
<xref rid="bibr20-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>
</sup> which classifies recommendations as strong (Grade 1 or ⊕) or weak (Grade 2 or ⊕⊕), according to the balance between benefits, risks, burden, and cost, and the degree of confidence in estimates of benefits, risks, and burden, and quality of evidence as high (Grade A), moderate (Grade B), or low (Grade C) according to factors including the risk of bias, precision of estimates, the consistency of the results, and the directness of the evidence as suggested by UpToDate.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr21-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>
</sup> References evaluated to score consensus statements in this document follow the statement directly and/or in succeeding paragraph(s) or sections.</p></sec><sec id="sec3-02683555211015887" disp-level="1"><title>US SOC meeting goals</title><list list-type="order" id="list1-02683555211015887"><list-item><p>Agree on a description of lipedema and a consensus SOC for the US.</p></list-item><list-item><p>Develop and publish clinical practice guidelines for use by providers, patients, and families.</p></list-item></list><p>This consensus standard of care guideline accomplishes Goal 1. Additional content is available online.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr22-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>
</sup> Consensus statements are graded to reflect the strength or weakness based on the current published evidence.</p></sec><sec id="sec4-02683555211015887" disp-level="1"><title>1.0 Lipedema overview</title><sec id="sec5-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><title>1.1 Lipedema should be regarded as a LCT disease versus a disease of just adipocytes (fat).<sup><xref rid="bibr11-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="bibr23-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">23</xref></sup> (⊕A)</title><p>Fat is a loose connective tissue. In addition to adipocytes, immune cells and fibroblasts, LCT has an extracellular matrix of fibers (e.g. collagen and elastin) that supports, protects, and connects tissues. Blood vessels and cells contribute fluid to the extracellular matrix. Fluid exits through lymphatic vessels<sup>
<xref rid="bibr24-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>
</sup> or remains in the tissue bound to glycosaminoglycans and proteoglycans. Glycosaminoglycans bind sodium and water due to their strong negative charge. Glycosaminoglycans increase when extracellular matrix water and/or salt increases.</p><p>When excess fluid is present, LCT becomes compliant,<sup>
<xref rid="bibr25-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>
</sup> allowing more fluid to collect, stimulating proteoglycan synthesis. Excess fluid limits cell access to oxygen resulting in hypoxia, inflammation and fibrosis.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr26-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>
</sup> Extracellular matrix fluid, free and bound to proteoglycans, also increases in lymphedema.<sup><xref rid="bibr27-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>,<xref rid="bibr28-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">28</xref></sup> When excess fluid collects in the extracellular matrix, it is called edema.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr29-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>
</sup></p></sec><sec id="sec6-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><title>1.2 Extracellular matrix edema in lipedema tissue is bound to proteoglycans. (⊕C)</title><p>Despite a lack of visible fluid in lipedema tissue on ultrasound,<sup>
<xref rid="bibr30-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>
</sup> extracellular fluid is higher in the tissue of women with lipedema compared to matched controls.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr31-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>
</sup> Sodium is also higher in the skin and LCT of women with lipedema.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr32-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>
</sup> Lipedema tissue has an enlarged extracellular matrix where proteoglycans reside.<sup><xref rid="bibr2-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="bibr33-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">33</xref></sup> In support, multiple proteoglycans are upregulated in excess adipose tissue in individuals with obesity.<sup>

<xref rid="bibr103-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">103</xref>

</sup> Certified lymphedema therapists may suggest modifications for compression garments, inelastic compression garments, “donning aides”, or adaptive equipment. </sup></p></sec><sec id="sec50-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>3.9.</label><title>Pneumatic compression devices stimulate lymphatic flow<sup>

<xref rid="bibr109-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">109</xref>

Surgical treatment</title><sec id="sec57-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><title>4.1. Lipedema reduction surgery is currently the only available technique for removing abnormal lipedema tissue such as adipocytes, nodules, fibrotic extracellular matrix, and other non-adipocyte components. It is also the only treatment that slows progression of lipedema and ideally would be performed before complications and disabilities from lipedema develop.<sup><xref rid="bibr110-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">110</xref>,<xref rid="bibr111-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">111</xref></sup> (⊕C)</title></sec><sec id="sec58-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><title>4.2. Lipedema reduction surgery utilizes suction lipectomy (liposuction), excision and manual extraction that spares blood and lymphatic vessels.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr17-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>
</sup> (⊕⊕C)</title><p>Lipedema reduction surgery significantly improves symptoms,<sup><xref rid="bibr110-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">110</xref>–<xref rid="bibr112-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">112</xref></sup> mobility, stance, gait,<sup>
<xref rid="bibr38-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>
</sup> valgus rotation/deformity of the knee and ankle, quality of life, and redistributes and restores the plantar arch.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr113-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">113</xref>
</sup> It also improves lymphatic symptoms, reducing the need for compression and manual therapy<sup><xref rid="bibr110-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">110</xref>–<xref rid="bibr112-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">112</xref>,<xref rid="bibr114-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">114</xref></sup> and improves lymphatic function as shown by radionucleotide lymphangioscintigraphy.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr115-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">115</xref>
</sup></p><p>The types of suction lipectomy recommended for people with lipedema are based around tumescent liposuction which uses a solution injected into the tissue to decrease pain and bleeding.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr116-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">116</xref>
</sup> Other mechanical methods can also be used such as Water Assisted Liposuction (WAL)<sup><xref rid="bibr114-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">114</xref>,<xref rid="bibr117-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">117</xref></sup> and Power Assisted Liposuction (PAL).<sup>
<xref rid="bibr118-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">118</xref>
</sup></p><p>To date, all studies showing clinical improvements for women with lipedema used tumescence or WAL techniques.<sup><xref rid="bibr110-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">110</xref>–<xref rid="bibr112-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">112</xref>,<xref rid="bibr119-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">119</xref></sup> There is little published information on the safety of laser or ultrasound technology for removing lipedema tissue.</p></sec><sec id="sec59-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>4.3.</label><title>Candidates for lipedema reduction surgery should generally be in good health</title><p>People with lipedema are different from the general population in that BMI is not a reliable indicator of overall health.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr17-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>
</sup> (⊕C)</p></sec><sec id="sec60-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>4.4.</label><title>There is no age limit for which people will benefit from lipedema reduction surgery.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr17-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>
</sup> (⊕C)</title></sec><sec id="sec61-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>4.5.</label><title>Indications for lipedema reduction surgery include a diagnosis of lipedema with demonstrated compliance and adherence to or failure of conservative therapies (3.0 Conservative and other therapies section ).<sup><xref rid="bibr13-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>–<xref rid="bibr17-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="bibr66-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">66</xref></sup> (⊕C)</title></sec><sec id="sec62-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>4.6.</label><title>Lipedema reduction surgery does not fit traditional volume limits for liposuction</title><p>Debulking lipedema tissue may require larger than traditional suction aspirate volumes<sup>
<xref rid="bibr120-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">120</xref>

This is not cosmetic liposuction as there are mobility, pain and health benefits when removing lipedema tissue.<sup><xref rid="bibr13-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>–<xref rid="bibr17-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">17</xref></sup> (⊕⊕B)</p></sec><sec id="sec63-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>4.7.</label><title>Women with lipedema should be treated with conservative therapy prior to lipedema reduction therapy (4.0 Surgical treatment section). (⊕⊕B)</title></sec><sec id="sec79-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>4.23.</label><title>Complete decongestive therapy is either no longer needed or the need reduced in people after recovery from lipedema reduction surgery.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr112-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">112</xref>
</sup> (⊕⊕A)</title></sec></sec><sec id="sec80-02683555211015887" disp-level="1"><label>5.0.</label><title>Arterial and venous disorders in lipedema</title><sec id="sec81-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.1.</label><title>The arterial and venous vascular status of people with lipedema should be evaluated.<sup><xref rid="bibr47-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">47</xref>,<xref rid="bibr121-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">121</xref></sup> (⊕⊕C)</title><p>Most people with lipedema have leg pain, all have leg swelling, either pitting or non-pitting, and many have underlying chronic venous disease.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr121-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>
</sup></p><p>The physical examination should include inspection and palpation of pulses in the limbs. Pulse palpation in people with lipedema may be difficult and painful due to limb size.</p></sec><sec id="sec82-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.2.</label><title>It is important to differentiate leg pain in lipedema from peripheral arterial disease. (⊕⊕A)</title><p>Peripheral arterial disease is common especially when major risk factors are present.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr130-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">130</xref>
</sup> Compression garments are a standard treatment for people with lipedema with signs of lymphatic impairment; however, compression garments are contraindicated for people with severe peripheral arterial disease.</p></sec><sec id="sec83-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.3.</label><title>If peripheral arterial disease is clinically suspected, ankle brachial index is recommended with whole leg or single segment (foot and ankle) assessment. (⊕A)</title><p>Arm or leg enlargement in lipedema may affect accuracy of the blood pressure measurements in this test and cause pain.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr131-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">131</xref>
</sup> If performing an ankle brachial index is not possible, measurement of a toe brachial index may be helpful. Forearm or wrist blood pressure measurement may be an alternative in this population. Other options include using a 4 MHz Doppler ultrasound probe (over the standard 8 MHz probe) and/or a larger blood pressure cuff.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr132-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">132</xref>
</sup></p><p>The arterial duplex ultrasound can eliminate the need for invasive procedures such as arteriography or computed tomography angiography.</p></sec><sec id="sec84-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.4.</label><title>Common venous conditions seen in people with lipedema include increased risk for venous thromboembolism and conditions associated with chronic venous disease: varicose veins, chronic venous insufficiency, and telangiectasias (spider veins).<sup>
<xref rid="bibr121-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>
</sup> (⊕C)</title></sec><sec id="sec85-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.5.</label><title>Lipedema, especially later stages, is associated with multiple comorbid conditions that increase the risk of venous thromboembolism, which includes superficial thrombophlebitis, deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr133-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">133</xref>
</sup> (⊕C)</title></sec><sec id="sec86-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.6.</label><title>Providers should perform a venous thromboembolism risk assessment score<sup>
<xref rid="bibr134-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">134</xref>
</sup> for people with lipedema and follow venous thromboembolism prophylaxis treatment guidelines. (⊕C)</title><p>Independent risk factors for venous thromboembolism based on Caprini risk stratification,<sup>
<xref rid="bibr134-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">134</xref>
</sup> especially for women with Stage 3 lipedema include:</p><list list-type="bullet" id="list2-02683555211015887"><list-item><p>BMI &gt;40 kg/m<sup>2</sup> (1 point)</p></list-item><list-item><p>Varicose veins (1 point)</p></list-item><list-item><p>Swollen legs, including loss of definition of bony prominences (1 point)</p></list-item><list-item><p>Decreased mobility<sup>
<xref rid="bibr113-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">113</xref>
</sup> (1 point) (⊕A)</p></list-item></list></sec><sec id="sec87-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.7.</label><title>Chronic venous disease can present with leg swelling and pain and should be considered in the differential diagnosis of lipedema. (⊕B)</title><p>Chronic venous disease is the most common vascular disorder in all populations. Chronic venous disease is the presence of morphological (i.e., venous dilation) or functional (e.g., venous reflux) abnormalities manifested by symptoms and/or signs indicating the need for further investigation or treatment. There is little data on lipedema and chronic venous disease. Two studies state ∼25% of women with lipedema have venous disease and one study showed 50% of women with lipedema and lipolymphedema had chronic venous insufficiency.<sup><xref rid="bibr53-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">53</xref>,<xref rid="bibr121-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>,<xref rid="bibr135-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">135</xref></sup> Lipedema and chronic venous disease often co-exist, share similar leg symptoms, and could exacerbate each other. Advancing age, female gender, and BMI compound an underlying relationship between lipedema and chronic venous disease.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr136-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">136</xref>
</sup></p><p>Symptoms of chronic venous disease include: leg pain, fatigue, heaviness, swelling, pruritus, restless legs and night cramps.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr137-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">137</xref>
</sup> The leg pain of chronic venous disease is generally worse with dependency and relieved by elevation. Symptoms of chronic venous disease are relieved by compression garments and walking. In people with lipedema, leg elevation does not improve swelling, and compression garments often cause pain.</p><p>The physical exam for chronic venous disease includes inspection of the arms and legs comparing each to the contralateral limb. Physical signs of chronic venous disease include telangiectasia, varicose veins, hyperpigmentation, erythema, inflammation, dryness, corona phlebectatica, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche and leg ulceration. Edema, pitting or non-pitting, should be noted.</p><p>Secondary lymphedema in people with lipedema can be difficult to evaluate. Not only does secondary lymphedema often occur in the absence of a positive Stemmer's sign,<sup>
<xref rid="bibr138-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">138</xref>
</sup> it requires palpation of tissue density and heaviness. As much as 0.5 L of fluid can be present in the calf/ankle before it is noticed. Women with any stage of lipedema may manifest lymphedema although it is more likely in more advanced stages.<sup><xref rid="bibr4-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>,<xref rid="bibr46-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">46</xref></sup></p></sec><sec id="sec88-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><title>5.8. The Clinical-Etiological-Anatomical-Pathophysiological (CEAP) classification for venous disease should be determined for people with lipedema.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr139-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">139</xref>
</sup> (⊕A)</title></sec><sec id="sec89-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.9.</label><title>The venous evaluation of people with lipedema includes a bilateral, lower extremity duplex ultrasound evaluation of the deep and superficial venous systems assessing for valvular insufficiency (reflux),<sup>
<xref rid="bibr121-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>
</sup> acute or chronic thrombosis<sup>
<xref rid="bibr133-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">133</xref>
</sup> and patterns of obstructive flow. (⊕A)</title><p>The scan should evaluate reflux in the superficial truncal veins (great saphenous, small saphenous and accessory saphenous), measure truncal vein diameters, and map large refluxing tributaries. These scans can be difficult to perform and assess in people with severe obesity, extensive lipedema, and lower extremity edema.</p><p>Duplex ultrasound may be helpful when the clinical examination for lipedema is unclear. For example, dermal thickness was normal in people with lipedema, while dermal thickness was increased and fluid was present in the loose connective tissue in cases of lymphedema.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr140-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">140</xref>
</sup></p><p>Knowing when to treat chronic venous disease in people with lipedema is challenging without published data. Generally, it is accepted to consider treatment of chronic venous disease when superficial truncal reflux is present, the symptoms interfere with activities of daily living, and people do not respond to conservative therapy (compression garments, manual therapy, 3.0 Conservative and other therapies section). (⊕B)</p></sec><sec id="sec90-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.10.</label><title>Providers should determine whether symptoms are from lipedema, chronic venous disease or both as they share many symptoms.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr121-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">121</xref>
</sup> (⊕B)</title><p>One goal for people with lipedema is to improve discomfort. It is important to give reasonable expectations for chronic venous disease treatment outcomes including that overall leg shape, edema, and underlying symptoms from lipedema most likely will not improve. However, by removing the chronic venous disease component, people can expect an overall net improvement of end of day symptoms.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr139-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">139</xref>
</sup></p></sec><sec id="sec91-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.11.</label><title>Consider thermal and non-thermal treatment modalities of chronic venous disease in people with lipedema.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr141-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">141</xref>
</sup> (⊕C)</title><p>There are two modalities for treating superficial truncal reflux in chronic venous disease: non-thermal and thermal. Non-thermal methods cause less inflammation, injury to adjacent structures, damage to adjacent lymphatics or nerves, risk of anesthetic complications, noxious needle punctures, and pre- and post-treatment discomfort. Thermal modalities are theoretically more likely to injure adjacent lymphatics; however, thermal ablation is more widely available and more effective on larger diameter veins.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr142-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">142</xref>
</sup></p></sec><sec id="sec92-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.12.</label><title>Due to alterations in lymphatic vessels in people with lipedema,<sup>
<xref rid="bibr46-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>
</sup> when thermal ablation is used to treat chronic venous disease in the proximal saphenous vein segments, generous peri-vascular tumescent anesthesia should be infiltrated, especially at the sapheno-femoral and sapheno-popliteal junctions, to increase protection of surrounding lymphatics.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr143-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">143</xref>
</sup> (⊕C)</title></sec><sec id="sec93-02683555211015887" disp-level="2"><label>5.13.</label><title>The decision of whether to treat distal saphenous segments and/or large tributaries of chronic venous disease in people with lipedema must be individualized.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr144-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">144</xref>
</sup> (⊕⊕C)</title></sec></sec><sec id="sec94-02683555211015887" disp-level="1"><title>Monitoring treatment</title><p>Though there is no lipedema specific health-related quality of life evaluation tool, several outcome instruments have been used to differentiate lipedema from lymphedema including the SF-36<sup>
<xref rid="bibr145-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">145</xref>
</sup> and the Patient Benefit Index.<sup>
<xref rid="bibr146-02683555211015887" ref-type="bibr">146</xref>
</sup></p></sec><sec id="sec95-02683555211015887" disp-level="1"><title>Research</title><p>How or why lipedema occurs is poorly understood, and for affected individuals, the signs and symptoms of progression remain unexplored. Key areas of research include:</p><list list-type="bullet" id="list3-02683555211015887"><list-item><p>Defining the penetrance of pain and its mechanisms,</p></list-item><list-item><p>Pathomechanism of muscle strength loss,</p></list-item><list-item><p>Connective tissue aspect of lipedema including hypermobile joints,</p></list-item><list-item><p>Difference and differential diagnosis between lipohypertrophy and lipedema,</p></list-item><list-item><p>Overall prevalence and incidence of lipedema as well as its demographic distribution.</p></list-item></list><p>Further research should focus on how to optimize treatment for people with lipedema, with a particular focus on patient quality of life, nutritional guidance, management of comorbid diseases, deeper tissue techniques to reduce inflammation and fibrosis, earlier diagnosis to allow for intervention and education, psychosocial support, as well as pre- and post-surgical protocols to improve care and assess medium- to long-term outcomes.</p></sec><sec id="sec96-02683555211015887" disp-level="1"><title>Conclusion</title><p>These findings are the consensus statements of US-based expert panelists put forth as a standard of care guideline for people with lipedema in the US. It is our goal and aspiration that that this guideline will improve the understanding of the loose connective tissue disease, lipedema, and that increased research and awareness of lipedema will advance our understanding towards increased diagnosis, improvements of treatments, and ultimately a cure for the community of affected individuals.</p></sec><sec id="ack1" sec-type="ack" disp-level="1"><title>Acknowledgements</title><p>The authors thank the Lipedema Foundation, the Fat Disorders Resource Society, Jaime Schwartz, and Ethan Larson for material support.</p></sec><sec id="fn-group1" sec-type="fn-group" disp-level="1"><fn-group><fn id="fn2"><p><bold>Declaration of Conflicting Interests:</bold> The author(s) declared the following potential conflicts of interest with respect to the research, authorship, and/or publication of this article: KLH received research funding from Raziel Therapeutics, is on the Speaker’s Board for Tactile Medical, and has received honorarium for speaking engagements from Sigvaris and Lymphapress.

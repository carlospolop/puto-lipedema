Abstract

The majority of disorders that cause renal potassium wasting present with abnormalities in adrenal hormone secretion. While these findings frequently lead patients to seek endocrine evaluation, clinicians often struggle to accurately diagnose these conditions, delaying treatment and adversely impacting patient care. At the same time, growing insight into the genetic and molecular basis of these disorders continues to improve their diagnosis and management. In this review, we outline a practical integrated approach to the evaluation of renal hypokalemia syndromes that are seen in endocrine practice while highlighting recent advances in understanding of the genetics and pathophysiology behind them.

Keywords: hypokalemia, genetics, Bartter, Gitelman, Liddle, aldosterone

Hypokalemia is a significant clinical problem, with estimates of prevalence as high as 11% in the general population, and evidence indicating that hypokalemia increases patient mortality (1, 2). While the differential diagnosis of hypokalemia is broad, encompassing reduced oral intake, transcellular ion shifts, gastrointestinal excretion, and diuretic-induced potassium loss, one important but often poorly recognized diagnostic category comprises the conditions that increase renal potassium excretion. Understanding these conditions is also important for the practicing endocrinologist, as most are either directly caused by disorders in adrenal regulation of renal potassium handling or indirectly lead to abnormalities of adrenal hormone secretion. Nonetheless, determining the cause of renal potassium wasting can be challenging. In this review, we first discuss the overall physiology behind renal potassium handling and the role of the adrenal gland in this process. We then provide an integrated and up-to-date approach to the diagnosis and management of these syndromes.

Physiology of Renal Potassium Handling

Reabsorption and secretion of potassium in the renal nephron is closely regulated to maintain normal circulating and cellular potassium levels. While the majority of filtered potassium is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule, urinary potassium excretion is largely determined by the degree of potassium secretion from alpha-intercalated cells in the collecting duct in conjunction with sodium and potassium reabsorption in the thick ascending limb (TAL) of the loop of Henle (3). In the TAL (Fig. 1A), sodium, potassium, and 2 chloride molecules are reabsorbed from the tubular lumen into the cell via the luminal sodium-potassium-2 chloride cotransporter (NKCC2). Chloride then leaves the cell through the basolateral chloride channels, mainly chloride channel Kb (CLCNKB) and to a lesser extent, CLCNKA (4). The basolateral Na-K-ATPase transports sodium out of the cell by actively moving 3 sodium molecules out and 2 potassium molecules in. Potassium is recycled into the tubular lumen through the potassium channel ROMK, which creates the necessary lumen-positive transepithelial potential for the uptake of cations such as calcium and magnesium through claudins (5).

In the distal convoluted tubule (DCT) (Fig. 1B), the luminal sodium-chloride cotransporter (NCC) transports sodium and chloride into the cell. Chloride leaves the cell through the basolateral CLCNKB while the basolateral Na-K ATPase exports sodium and imports potassium (6). Additionally, calcium and magnesium are absorbed via the transcellular route in the DCT. Calcium enters through the luminal TRPV5 and leaves via the basolateral Na/Ca exchanger, which exchanges calcium for sodium. Magnesium enters the cell via TRPM6 and exits through the basolateral Na/Mg exchanger (7, 8). In the principal cells of the collecting duct (Fig. 1C), the amiloride-sensitive epithelial sodium channel (ENaC), allows for apical sodium entry, generating a negative potential difference in the tubular lumen and allowing for passive potassium movement into the lumen through apical potassium channels (ROMK and BK). Importantly, distal delivery of sodium is critical for determining the degree of potassium secretion by the principal cells as it promotes sodium absorption which in turn increases the electrochemical gradient driving potassium secretion (9).

Aldosterone is produced by adrenal zona glomerulosa cells (ZG) and is the primary endocrine factor in regulation of renal sodium and potassium homeostasis. ZG cells utilize the Na+/K+-ATPase to maintain a potassium gradient across their cell membrane. Cell depolarization then leads to increased intracellular calcium through the opening of voltage-gated calcium channels, which in turn acts via calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinases to both upregulate transport of cholesterol substrate for steroid synthesis and increase transcription of the aldosterone synthase enzyme, CYP11B2 (10). The 2 primary stimuli for aldosterone synthesis are angiotensin II as part of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) and increased plasma potassium. Angiotensin II production increases in response to hypovolemia or reduced sodium concentration and binds to ZG cells at the angiotensin II subtype 1 receptor (AT1R). AT1R then engages in multiple downstream signaling pathways to depolarize the cell and trigger the opening of the voltage-gated calcium channels, leading to increased CYP11B2 synthesis. Increases in extracellular potassium, likewise, reduce the membrane electrochemical gradient, depolarizing the cell with the same result (10, 11). Additionally, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) can stimulate aldosterone production. This occurs acutely through the binding of ACTH to the melanocortin type 2 receptor (MC2R), leading to overall upregulation of steroidogenesis (12). Chronically, continuous ACTH exposure causes a decline in aldosterone levels whereas pulsatile ACTH stimulates persistent elevations in aldosterone production (13). Notably, patients with primary aldosteronism (PA) have greater increases in aldosterone in response to ACTH than healthy controls, independent of angiotensin II, and have greater ZG expression of MC2R, suggesting that ACTH also has a role in abnormal aldosterone secretion (14, 15).

In the kidney, aldosterone binds to the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) in collecting duct principal cells which increases the activity and expression of the luminal ENaC sodium channel and ROMK potassium channel (16). At the same time, aldosterone upregulates principal cell basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase activity, increasing the electrochemical gradient that drives sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion across these ion channels (17, 18).

Differential Diagnosis and Integrated Approach

An integrated approach to diagnosing causes of renal hypokalemia is shown in Fig. 2. The clinician should first exclude the use of potassium-wasting diuretics as well as nonrenal causes of hypokalemia, which can be done through measurement of urinary potassium excretion. In the following sections we will review the differential diagnosis for each major category in this algorithm and highlight the distinguishing features of each disorder.

Patients Without Hypertension

The first major step in evaluation is to distinguish patients with and without hypertension. Renal potassium wasting in the absence of hypertension raises the concern for a group of renal tubulopathies including Bartter Syndrome (BS), Gitelman Syndrome (GS), and EAST syndrome (EAST). All of these conditions share the common pathophysiology of impaired sodium and chloride absorption in the TAL or DCT. The result is upregulation of the juxtaglomerular apparatus, causing secondary hyperaldosteronism along with greater distal sodium delivery. This leads to increased sodium-potassium exchange in the collecting duct and greater potassium excretion. Findings common to all of these disorders thus include hypokalemia with hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis as well as hypertrophy of the juxtaglomerular apparatus with elevated levels of renin and aldosterone (19).

Bartter Syndrome, Gitelman Syndrome, and EAST

Bartter syndrome (BS) (Fig. 3) is an inherited renal tubular disorder caused by mutations in TAL ion transporters. At least 7 genetic mutations causing BS have been identified, producing 5 types of BS with variable clinical presentations. BS types I-IV are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, while BS type V follows an X-linked inheritance pattern. Type I BS, also known as antenatal BS, is caused by mutations in the SLC12A1 gene encoding the NKCC2 protein in the TAL, causing reduced sodium chloride absorption (20). Type II BS is caused by heterozygote loss of function mutations in the KCNJ1 gene encoding the potassium channel ROMK a potassium channel that maintains the potassium concentration in the lumen and sodium chloride reabsorption in the TAL (21). Type III BS, the most common type, is caused by mutations in the CLCNKB gene encoding CLC-Kb, which is responsible for chloride reabsorption in the TAL and DCT (22). Type IV BS is the result of inactivating mutations affecting the CLC-Kb and CLC-Ka channels, loss of Cl− transport in the TAL, and impaired reabsorption of sodium and chloride (23). Type V BS is caused by mutations in the MAGED2 gene, reducing the reabsorption of sodium and chloride by NKCC2 (24). Finally, autosomal dominant hypocalcemia (ADH) with BS is caused by activating mutations in the CASR gene which encodes the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR). This leads to increased expression of the CaSR protein. The CaSR activating mutation decreases KCNJ1, NKCC2, and Na + -K + - ATPase, causing a phenotype similar to type I BS (25).

The prevalence of BS is estimated to be around 1 in 1 000 000 individuals and most often presents in infancy, although some individuals, particularly those with type III BS, can present as late as adulthood (26). Key distinguishing features of BS are noted to be similar to patients on loop diuretics. The TAL is essential for urinary concentration, so patients experience a defect in concentrating urine, typically isosthenuria or occasionally hyposthenuria (27). Loss of the lumen-positive voltage gradient also leads to impaired cation uptake, resulting in variable degrees of hypercalciuria. Urinary electrolyte loss seen in type III BS may be less severe than in type I and type II due to compensation by other chloride transport channels in the renal tubule (28). Both renal salt loss and local release of angiotensin result in increased activity on cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) causing excessive prostaglandin E2 production. Nephrocalcinosis may also be present, although one study reported it in only 20% of patients with type III BS (22). Type IV BS is associated with sensorineural hearing loss, as CLC-Ka also exists in the cochlea (23). Additional perinatal findings include polyhydramnios, preterm birth, and growth retardation which persists into later life (22, 24, 26, 29).

Gitelman syndrome (GS) (Fig. 4) is also an inherited renal tubular disease with autosomal recessive inheritance. GS is considered one of the most common hereditary renal tubular diseases with prevalence approximately 1 in 40 000 to 1 in 4000 individuals, with slightly higher prevalence observed in Asian populations (30). In one study, the prevalence in the Japanese population was estimated to be 10.3/10 000 to 2.3/1000 (31) GS is caused by mutations in the SLC12A3 gene that encodes NCC, and CLCNKB, the gene for the Kb chloride channel in the DCT. Notably, as many as 45% of cases may actually be due to compound heterozygote mutations (32). More than 400 different mutation sites have been identified, with the majority being missense mutations (33).

Incomplete NCC function leads to a reduction in sodium and chloride reabsorption in the DCT, resulting in hypovolemia, elevation of renin and aldosterone, hypokalemia, and hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, similar to BS. However, the electrolyte changes in GS are more similar to patients on thiazide diuretics. Unlike BS, tubuloglomerular feedback and the ability to absorb calcium in the TAL remains intact in GS, causing hypocalciuria due to a compensatory increase in salt and calcium reabsorption in the proximal tubule (34-36). GS also presents with hypomagnesemia, hypothesized to be due to decreased expression of the TRPM6 magnesium channel in the DCT, loss of membrane potential, or DCT atrophy (37, 38). Hypomagnesemia is typically not present in BS though it has been reported in BS type III (39). The symptoms of GS are also milder than those of BS because the DCT accounts for less than 10% of total filtration and so symptoms are often recognized later in life, with GS patients typically experiencing normal growth (19, 40).

EAST is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in KCNJ10, which encodes the Kir4.1 basolateral potassium channel of the DCT. EAST syndrome presents with a tubulopathy and electrolyte abnormalities similar to GS. However, the disorder is also characterized by neurologic features giving it the acronym EAST: epilepsy (E), ataxia (A), sensorineural deafness (S), and renal salt-wasting tubulopathy due to defective distal tubular function (T) (41).

Diagnosis of BS, GS, and EAST

While BS and GS can often be differentiated based on their electrolyte findings, there can be some overlap, such as with hypomagnesemia in BS type III.

The main distinguishing characteristic is the presence of hypocalciuria and hypercalciuria in BS. Additionally, impaired growth is seen only in BS, which is usually diagnosed at an early age, whereas the age of diagnosis for GS and severity of symptoms is variable. Prostaglandin levels are also generally normal in GS patients. EAST can be differentiated from GS based on specific neurologic findings. Diuretics tests are sometimes used to demonstrate the lack of loop diuretic effects in BS and thiazide diuretics in GS (42).

While these tests can be useful for BS type 1 and GS patients, their usefulness for other types of BS is limited (43). Additionally, these tests can produce serious adverse effects, such as severe volume depletion, and there is a wide variability in the response to diuretics in GS that can lead to misinterpretation.

Genetic studies are the most conclusive tool for diagnosing BS and GS, as their high phenotypic variability can lead to confusion. Besides diagnosing the disease, genetic studies also offer valuable information on treatment strategies, comorbidities, and enable prenatal diagnosis (44). Massive sequencing technologies such as ultra-sequencing or next-generation sequencing (NGS) and identifying copy number variations (CNVs) can now be utilized to more definitely diagnose GS.

Treatment of BS, GS, and EAST

The treatment for BS and GS is primarily symptomatic, involving the correction of hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis, inhibition of RAS activity, and inhibition of excessive synthesis of prostaglandin E2 in BS (45, 46). Potassium supplementation is a common approach for correcting hypokalemia in patients with BS and GS, but it can be challenging to normalize serum electrolytes when there is an ongoing tubular leak. The optimal dosing frequency is uncertain, and serum levels are greatly affected by the timing of the last dose. The use of amiloride, triamterene, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) to blockade the secondary hyperaldosteronism that is present in these conditions has limitations. While these medications can increase serum potassium levels and partially correct hypomagnesemia, they can also aggravate hypercalciuria, salt-wasting, and cause volume depletion. Magnesium chloride is used to treat hypomagnesemia in GS patients. It can also help correct hypokalemia, acid-base imbalances, and hypocalciuria. However, diarrhea is a potential side effect.

Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors can be effective in inhibiting RAS, reducing aldosterone secretion, and improving hypokalemia (47). However, it is important to consider potential adverse reactions such as hypotension. Prostaglandin synthase inhibitors prevent excessive synthesis of prostaglandin E2 and result in better blood pressure control and electrolyte balance (48). It is important to monitor for potential side effects, such as reduced glomerular filtration rate, ulcers, necrotizing colitis, and gastrointestinal perforations in infants. A recent study (49) demonstrated the potential benefits of acetazolamide, a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, as an adjunct therapy in the treatment of BS. Finally, in an animal study, gene therapy was used to target the NKCC2 gene in the TAL. The vector was injected into the renal artery and designed to target the assigned nephron segment. Exogenous and endogenous NKCC2 were co-expressed in the TAL, showing potential for gene therapy as a treatment for tubulopathies like BS and GS. However, more research is needed to ensure safety and permanent transfection (50).

Patients With Hypertension

Conditions of renal hypokalemia and hypertension are termed low renin hypertension and can be broadly divided based on aldosterone level. High aldosterone indicates primary aldosteronism (PA) which is by far the most common disorder in this group. Conversely, aldosterone suppression signifies pseudohyperaldosteronism, a group of conditions of endogenous or exogenous etiology, characterized by either increased stimulation of the MR, as seen in apparent mineralocorticoid excess (AME), hypercortisolism, and certain forms of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), or by autonomous ENaC activity found in Liddle Syndrome (LS).

Primary Aldosteronism

PA is defined as excess aldosterone production that is autonomous from RAS stimulation. In the kidney, this results in increased stimulation of the MR and ENaC, leading to sodium retention, potassium excretion, and increased intravascular volume. The predominant feature of PA is hypertension with estimates of prevalence ranging from 5% to 10% in hypertensive patients to as high as 20% in patients with resistant hypertension (51, 52). However, hypokalemia occurs in only a relative minority of cases, comprising 9% to 37% of all patients with PA (51). PA is divided into subtypes based on adrenal morphology. Historically, these have included unilateral aldosterone-producing adenoma and bilateral idiopathic adrenal hyperplasia (51). However, it is now clear that PA can result from a range of additional histopathologic presentations. These include aldosterone-producing adenomas (APA), aldosterone-producing nodules (APN) which are < 1 cm in size and distinct on histology, and aldosterone-producing micronodules (APM), which are indistinct clusters of cells beneath the adrenal capsule which stain positive for expression of the CYP11B2 (53).

Both germline and somatic mutations cause PA. Familial hyperaldosteronism (FH) from germline mutations makes up only about 2% of patients with PA, whereas somatic mutations are much more common, accounting for over 90% of unilateral PA (51, 54, 55). There are 5 subtypes of FH, all with autosomal dominant inheritance (Fig. 5). FH-I derives from a chimeric gene fusing the ACTH responsive promoter region for CYP11B1 with the coding region of CYP11B2 (56). This results in “glucocorticoid remediable hyperaldosteronism,” in which ACTH drives excess aldosterone production, which can thus be suppressed by administration of glucocorticoid (57). FH subtypes II (CLCN2 mutation) and III (KCNJ5 mutation) involve mutations in ZG cell membrane chloride and potassium channels, respectively, which increase membrane ion permeability, leading to cell membrane depolarization, increased calcium entry, and upregulation of CYP11B2 (58, 59). In FH-IV (CACNA1H mutation) and a fifth subtype (CACNA1D mutation), called PASNA due to accompanying seizures and neurologic abnormalities, activating mutations in membrane calcium channels directly increase intracellular calcium (60, 61).

Somatic mutations have been reported for the same genes as FH, with the exception of FH-I, but are also found in the Na+/K+ATPase (ATP1A1) and Ca2+ ATPase (ATP2B3), causing increased Na+ and H+ permeability and calcium influx, respectively (62, 63). Other somatic mutations in β-catenin (CTNNB1) increase glomerulosa cell proliferation while mutations in GNA11 and GNAQ, which mediate the glomerulosa cell response to angiotensin II, frequently occur alongside CTNNB1 mutations and have an additive effect on aldosterone secretion (64). Additional recently reported somatic mutations include SLC30A1, affecting zinc transporter ZNT1 and leading to increased sodium influx and ZG cell depolarization, and CADM1, which increases aldosterone synthase activity through impaired activity of intercellular gap junctions (65, 66). It has recently been shown that in cases with multiple aldosterone-producing lesions within the same gland, different mutations are often present within each lesion, suggesting that multiple mutational events may contribute (54, 55, 67).

In terms of clinical presentation, aldosterone-producing adenomas due to KCNJ5 mutations tend to be larger and may be associated with higher aldosterone levels, more severe hypokalemia, and increased abdominal aortic wall thickness (68-70) while, in a study of young patients with PA, smaller aldosterone-producing nodules more frequently harbored CACNA1D mutations (55). Finally, with KCNJ5 mutations, overall the most common genotype in patients with unilateral secretion, it has been proposed that patients with bilateral secretion may have a different genetic profile. Analysis of adrenal tissue from one group of patients with bilateral secretion found that 58% of lesions contained CACNA1D mutations while only 1% harbored KCNJ5 mutations (71). However, these results need to be interpreted cautiously as patients with bilateral secretion do not routinely undergo surgical resection and therefore the sample of patients in this study was small and possibly subject to bias.

Diagnosis of PA

Diagnosis of PA currently relies on a complex multistep diagnostic algorithm that utilizes the ratio of plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC) to plasma renin activity (PRA) (or direct renin concentration [DRC] in some centers) for screening as well as confirmatory testing involving suppression of PAC (51). However, these measures can be affected by factors such as patient medications, while recent data has demonstrated troubling variability in measurements of PAC within the same individual (72). In addition, determining PA subtype in terms of unilateral vs bilateral secretion at present requires adrenal venous sampling, which is invasive and highly operator dependent.

As a result, there has been increasing interest in noninvasive methods utilizing peripheral blood findings coupled with genetic applications to reliably diagnose and subtype PA. For example, a specific DNA methylation “signature” may distinguish PA from primary hypertension as well as other forms of secondary hypertension like hypercortisolism and pheochromocytoma (73).

In terms of subtyping, plasma steroid profile correlates with genetic mutation and PA subtype. Aldosterone-producing lesions with KCNJ5 mutations are associated with higher plasma levels of hybrid steroids such as 18-oxocortisol and 18-hydroxycortisol (54, 74) and one model using plasma steroid profile was able to predict genetic mutation with 95% accuracy (75). Similarly, analysis with machine learning has been able to predict unilateral disease due to KCNJ5 mutations with 85% sensitivity and 97% specificity (76). While these methods are still not a replacement for adrenal venous sampling, they do represent a potentially powerful tool for diagnosing and characterizing patients with PA in a way that may indicate which patients would be candidates for surgical resection or medical management.

Management of PA

To this point, therapy has largely consisted of either adrenalectomy for patients with confirmed unilateral disease or the use of mineralocorticoid antagonists in patients with bilateral disease or who are not candidates for surgery. Recently, the emergence of aldosterone synthase inhibitors holds promise as an additional therapeutic option (77). Recent advances also indicate that knowledge of the genetic basis of PA in specific patients may guide approaches to therapy. Somatic KCNJ5 mutations in unilateral disease are a significant predictor of surgical outcomes, including remission of hypertension, reduction in the number of required antihypertensive medications, resolution of hypokalemia, and improvements in left ventricular hypertrophy and aortic wall thickness (70, 78-80). Conversely, at least one study has suggested that CACNA1D mutations are more common in patients who do not attain surgical cure, although it should be noted that as many as 30% of the patients in this study who failed to gain remission had KCNJ5 mutations (81). Nonetheless, these findings, taken together with the aforementioned advances in steroidomics, suggest that noninvasive diagnostic methods may provide future tools for recommending patient therapy.

CAH With 17α-Hydroxylase Deficiency and 11β-Hydroxylase Deficiency

In hypertensive hypokalemia, CAH should be suspected in the absence of PA where there are elevated mineralocorticoid precursors or abnormal sexual development. 17α-Hydroxylase deficiency (17OHD) is an autosomal recessive condition due to a mutation in the CYP17A1 gene, which codes for the p450c17 enzyme. This disorder is quite rare, accounting for fewer than 1% of cases of CAH (82). P450c17 carries out both the 17-alpha hydroxylation of pregnenolone and progesterone to 17-hydroxy pregnenolone and 17-hydroxyprogesterone, respectively, as well as the 17,20 lyase activity that converts 17-hydroxy pregnenolone to DHEA and 17-hydroxy progesterone to androstenedione (83). Its loss affects both the adrenal and gonads, resulting in deficient glucocorticoid and sex steroid production with elevations in mineralocorticoid precursors, including deoxycorticosterone and corticosterone (84-86). Excess deoxycorticosterone stimulates the MR, leading to salt retention, hypertension, and hypokalemia. Lack of sex steroids produces ambiguous genitalia in 46,XY individuals and absence of secondary sexual characteristics with amenorrhea and infertility in 46,XX patients (87).

11β-Hydroxylase deficiency (11OHD) is an autosomal recessive condition resulting from mutation of the CYP11B1 gene on chromosome 8. It is more common that 17OHD, accounting for close to 8% of CAH (82, 88). 11OHD reduces conversion of deoxycorticosterone to corticosterone and 11-deoxycortisol to cortisol, causing reduction in both cortisol and aldosterone, with increased levels of mineralocorticoid precursors such as deoxycorticosterone and heightened androgen production. Classic and nonclassic forms are defined based on severity. About two-thirds of patients have hypertension and hypokalemia is variable (89). XX individuals present with ambiguous genitalia and virilization in classic disease (90) or symptoms of hyperandrogenism such as hirsutism and acne in milder disease. Both XX and XY patients may experience precocious puberty, and additional clinical features include hyperpigmentation and short stature (90, 91). Laboratory testing shows elevated deoxycorticosterone, 11-deoxycortisol, and androgens, while cortisol and aldosterone are suppressed to varying degrees. Renin activity is low due to the mineralocorticoid activity of deoxycorticosterone while ACTH is high. Unlike 17OHD, however, corticosterone is decreased.

Diagnosis of 17OHD and 11OHD

Laboratory findings in these conditions include low renin and cortisol with elevated ACTH. Aldosterone levels are typically low but can vary in both disorders (88, 89, 92-95). A key differentiating factor between 17OHD and 11OHD is that 17OHD presents with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism while 11OHD demonstrates androgen excess. Additionally, deoxycorticosterone is high in both while corticosterone is elevated only in 17OHD and 11-deoxycortisol only in 11OHD. Historically, diagnosis has been established through ACTH-stimulated rise in steroid precursors (96). Definitive diagnosis can now be made through genetic testing for variants, although around 100 different mutations have been described for each condition (88, 97).

Management of 17OHD and 11OHD

The mainstay of treatment for both conditions is administration of glucocorticoid to reduce ACTH-driven synthesis of mineralocorticoid precursors, although overtreatment can result in mineralocorticoid deficiency in 11OHD (98). Glucocorticoid also reduces androgen production in 11OHD while patients with 17OHD usually require sex steroid supplementation. Some patients may also require mineralocorticoid antagonists to fully control blood pressure and correct hypokalemia as well as calcium channel blockers for hypertension (93). Bilateral adrenalectomy has also been used in 11OHD as a measure of last resort (99).

Liddle Syndrome, Apparent Mineralocorticoid Excess Syndrome, and Syndrome of Ectopic ACTH

These 3 conditions all present with hypokalemic hypertension in the absence of excess aldosterone or other mineralocorticoid precursors.

Liddle syndrome (LS) is an autosomal dominant condition caused by germline mutations in one or more of the SCNN1A, SCNN1B, and SCNN1G genes which encode each of the 3 subunits of ENaC (100). LS is, however, quite rare, with reported prevalence of only 1.52% among hypertensive patients under age 40 who have no other identified secondary cause of hypertension (101). LS is characterized by autonomous increased activity of ENaC in the renal collecting duct. This results in greater sodium and water reabsorption, with concomitant increases in potassium excretion leading to downregulation of renin and aldosterone secretion (102). Potassium in particular is lost via the creation of a luminal potential difference due to the movement of sodium out of the tubular lumen and flow of potassium through ROMK and BK transporters into the tubular lumen. Similar to PA, these patients also demonstrate metabolic alkalosis due to enhanced ammoniagenesis by the proximal tubule, increased H+ excretion by alpha-intercalated, and downregulation of HCO3− secretion in beta-intercalated cells in the collecting duct (103). These patients thus present with early-onset hypertension and hypokalemia, accompanied by low PRA and PAC, causing this condition to be labeled as pseudohyperaldosteronism (104).

Apparent mineralocorticoid excess syndrome (AME), in its inherited form, is an autosomal recessive condition caused by mutations in HD11B2, the gene encoding 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 2 (11HSD2), which converts cortisol to biologically inactive cortisone (105). This defect in conversion results in excess cortisol access to the MR, producing enhanced renal sodium reabsorption via ENaC, and increased excretion of potassium and hydrogen ions. These patients also present with hypertension, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis and low aldosterone (106). Multiple 11HSD2 mutations have been reported, with one recent study finding 54 mutations, either homozygous or compound heterozygous, in a population of 101 patients with AME (107). The phenotype of AME varies in severity based on the extent to which different mutations impair the activity of 11HSD2, leading to a division of AME cases into classic and nonclassic forms. Classic AME patients usually present in early childhood with severe hypertension, hypokalemia, alkalosis, and low birthweight, whereas nonclassic AME can present later in life with only mild hypertension (108). AME is rare, though the overall prevalence of AME remains unclear and recent studies have suggested that nonclassic AME is as prevalent as 7% in some populations (109). Of note, acquired forms of AME, mostly caused by exposure to compounds that inhibit 11HSD2, are actually more common than inherited forms. Glycyrrhetinic acid in licorice inhibits 11HSD2, although evidence has suggested it may also act as an agonist at the MR (110, 111). Similarly, naringenin in grapefruit can inhibit 11HSD2 (112). Certain pharmaceuticals, most notably some azole antifungal agents, have also been implicated in AME (113).

Similarly, hypercortisolism can overwhelm 11HSD2, leading to MR activation and hypokalemia (114). While endogenous hypercortisolism is most often due to ACTH-producing pituitary adenomas, or autonomous adrenal cortisol production, hypokalemia is most common in ectopic ACTH production, where the presence of hypokalemia correlates with the degree of hypercortisolism (115). Bronchial and thymic carcinoids are the most common source of ectopic ACTH though other malignancies including medullary thyroid carcinoma, small cell lung carcinoma, and neuroendocrine tumors of the pancreas and adrenal have been implicated (115). The majority of ectopic ACTH-producing tumors are sporadic. However, there are reports of ACTH secretion from thymic carcinomas in patients with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1, Von-Hippel-Lindau syndrome, and neurofibromatosis type 1 and medullary thyroid carcinomas in multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (116-119).

Diagnosis of LS, AME, and Syndrome of Ectopic ACTH Production

LS may be distinguished from AME by a lack of response to MRAs and by 24-hour urine cortisol-cortisone ratio, which is normal in LS but elevated in AME (104, 120, 121). The definitive diagnosis of LS is made by genetic testing. As of the time of this writing, several clinical tests are available on the Genetic Testing Registry (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gtr/conditions/C0221043/), a central location for voluntary submission of genetic test information by providers, the majority of which are “sequence analysis of the entire coding region” (122). More recently, it has been found that urinary exosome levels of 11HSD2 mRNA is also a potential biomarker of AME and there is ongoing research into whether nonclassic AME has an identifiable metabolomics profile (123, 124). Testing for hypercortisolism is usually prompted by clinical features of glucocorticoid excess and consists of cortisol suppression testing, measurement of urinary free cortisol, or late-night salivary cortisol (125, 126). Ectopic ACTH production can be differentiated from a pituitary source using inferior petrosal sinus sampling, although the corticotropin-releasing hormone stimulation test has been used when available in some centers (127). A combination of computed tomography and functional imaging should be used to localize the source of ectopic ACTH (128).

Management of LS, AME, and Syndrome of Ectopic ACTH Production

As ENaC activity in LS is independent of mineralocorticoid activity, therapy involves direct inhibition of ENaC through amiloride or other potassium-sparing diuretics, such as triamterene (104). In contrast, MRAs are the central component of therapy in AME and are effective at reducing blood pressure, sometimes in combination with amiloride or triamterene (129, 130). Dexamethasone combined with MRAs has been reported in isolated cases to have additional benefit in AME through suppression of cortisol, but this carries the risk of complications from glucocorticoid excess (130, 131).

Definitive treatment for ectopic ACTH production is tumor resection, although bilateral adrenalectomy remains an option in extreme cases (132). When cure cannot be achieved through surgery, steroidogenesis inhibitors can be utilized including ketoconazole, levoketoconazole, metyrapone, osilodrostat, mitotane, and etomidate (133, 134). Metyrapone and osilodrostat can, however, cause accumulation of mineralocorticoid precursors leading to hypertension and hypokalemia, which can be mitigated with potassium supplementation and MRAs (135, 136). The glucocorticoid receptor antagonist mifepristone is also effective, although patients remain susceptible to hypokalemia from excess cortisol (137). Although a full review of therapy for ectopic ACTH syndromes is beyond the scope of this paper, additional strategies include the use of somatostatin analogues, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and tumor radiofrequency ablation (138).

Conclusion

Hypokalemia remains a common problem in endocrine practice, with significant implications for overall health outcomes. Renally mediated hypokalemia can be a cause or an effect of adrenal hormone abnormalities. As many of these pathologic conditions are relatively rare, their recognition is challenging and often missed, causing delay in their management. Understanding the pathophysiologic basis of these conditions and using a systematic approach can therefore be helpful in identifying patients with an underlying endocrine-associated cause of hypokalemia and determining appropriate treatment.

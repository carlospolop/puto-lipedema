BS results from mutations affecting ion transporters involved in salt reabsorption in the thick ascending limb of Henle's loop ( 3 ). In contrast, pseudo-Bartter syndrome (pseudo-BS) presents with similar biochemical features but occurs secondary to acquired conditions, such as chronic vomiting, diuretic or laxative abuse, cystic fibrosis, or eating disorders, such as anorexia nervosa. Pseudo-BS without an apparent external cause presents a diagnostic challenge, particularly when signs of volume depletion are subtle. An under-recognized mechanism involves localized renal ischemia due to focal scarring, often a sequela of recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs). Such scarring may provoke localized renin release and subsequent activation of the intrarenal renin-angiotensin system, leading to elevation of aldosterone levels, even when systemic blood pressure remains preserved. We herein report a middle-aged woman with persistent hypokalemia, intermittent edema, and a history of recurrent UTIs. Abdominal imaging showed focal renal scarring consistent with localized ischemia and inappropriate renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation. This case highlights a diagnostic blind spot, pseudo-BS, driven by aberrant RAAS activation secondary to renal scarring. It also illustrates a clinical paradox, wherein restricting salt and water to prevent edema may paradoxically worsen the condition by enhancing RAAS activity. Case Report A 40-year-old Japanese woman was referred for the evaluation of persistent hypokalemia, bilateral lower extremity edema, and episodic limb weakness. She had no history of developmental delays or familial electrolyte disturbances. Her body weight had gradually declined from 53 kg (body mass index; BMI 22 kg/m 2 ) in her 20s to 42 kg (BMI 17.6 kg/m 2 ) at presentation, following a 10-kg postpartum loss and ongoing undernutrition. She reported frequent nocturnal vomiting and generalized fatigue throughout her 30s and had a long-standing history of recurrent UTIs since childhood. These episodes had been untreated during her early years, and no urological evaluation had been conducted. She denied any use of diuretics, laxatives, or weight loss agents, including over-the-counter supplements. Her diet was chronically low in animal proteins and fish, and she had minimal sun exposure. As a physical therapist, she frequently experienced orthostatic dizziness during work. Approximately four years prior to admission, she had been diagnosed with dyslipidemia and was started on statin therapy. Routine follow-up laboratory tests subsequently revealed persistent hypokalemia (1.9-2.6 mmol/L), particularly during warmer months, which was accompanied by fatigue and muscle weakness. Although potassium chloride was prescribed, poor palatability resulted in poor adherence and suboptimal correction. Three years before admission, she visited a general hospital, where testing revealed markedly elevated plasma renin activity (PRA ≥20 ng/mL/h) and plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC 325 pg/mL) despite consistently low blood pressure (e.g. 95/54 mmHg). Owing to the difficulty in tolerating potassium chloride, multiple mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) were prescribed sequentially; however, hypokalemia persisted along with sustained urinary potassium wasting. In addition, the patient reported occasionally skipping doses of MRAs around the time of her menstrual period because of low blood pressure symptoms. The patient was admitted to our hospital for a further evaluation. On admission, her BMI was 17.3 kg/m 2 , indicating underweight status; however, there was no evidence of limb muscle weakness or peripheral edema, and no calluses on the dorsum of the hand (“Russell's sign”) suggestive of habitual vomiting were observed. Laboratory data revealed profound hypokalemia (K 2.4 mmol/L), low serum chloride (Cl 90 mmol/L), alkalemia (pH 7.46), and elevated bicarbonate (HCO 3 - 39.8 mmol/L) and PCO 2 (56.5 mmHg), indicating metabolic alkalosis with concurrent respiratory acidosis ( Table 1 ). Despite these abnormalities, serum sodium, calcium, and magnesium levels were within the normal range, and there was no evidence of hypomagnesemia or hypocalciuria, suggestive of Gitelman syndrome (GS). The renal function was preserved, with a creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 81.0 mL/min/1.73 m 2 and a cystatin C-based eGFR of 76.0 mL/min/1.73 m 2 . Urinary electrolyte analysis revealed inappropriately high renal potassium wasting, with a fractional excretion of potassium (FEK) of 30.3%. A urinary chloride concentration greater than 20 mmol/L argued against extrarenal chloride loss, such as vomiting, and instead suggested a renal cause of salt wasting, as seen in BS or pseudo-BS states. To assess volume responsiveness, isotonic saline infusion (1 L/day for 3 consecutive days) was administered. As shown in Table 2 , this resulted in a gradual correction of electrolyte and acid-base disturbances, with serum chloride increasing from 94 to 107 mmol/L, potassium from 2.4 to 3.1 mmol/L, and uric acid from 11.6 to 6.1 mg/dL. Metabolic alkalosis also improved, with venous pH decreasing from 7.436 to 7.388 and bicarbonate from 36.2 to 28.1 mmol/L. These changes, along with the normalization of FEK, indicated that chronic hypovolemia contributed significantly to RAAS activation. Furthermore, to assess RAAS responsiveness, furosemide stimulation and captopril suppression tests were performed ( Table 3 ). The furosemide test revealed a modest increase in PRA (＜0.2 → 1.1 ng/mL/h) accompanied by a marked elevation in PAC (25.3 → 318 pg/mL). This disproportionate aldosterone response relative to the minimal rise in renin suggests enhanced adrenal sensitivity to the renin-angiotensin axis. In contrast, the captopril challenge test demonstrated an appropriate suppression of PAC (98.4 → 27.8 pg/mL), indicating preserved feedback regulation of aldosterone secretion. Meanwhile, PRA showed only a modest increase (0.5 → 1.4 ng/mL/h) without the marked renin surge typically observed in renovascular hypertension. The pathophysiology appeared to involve focal renin release and activation driven by ischemia in scarred regions of the right kidney under conditions of volume contraction. Genetic testing for hereditary tubulopathies (e.g. SLC12A3 and CLCNKB) was performed in view of the acquired nature of her condition and favorable response to supportive therapy. MRA therapy was discontinued owing to orthostatic intolerance. Oral potassium chloride (16 mEq/day) was resumed with improved adherence, and nutritional counseling was provided. The patient's clinical course is summarized in Fig. 2 , showing gradual normalization of potassium, bicarbonate, PRA, and PAC levels. Intermittent limb muscle weakness improved with potassium supplementation. On admission, no peripheral edema was evident, although she had previously experienced mild intermittent swelling of the face and legs. These episodes were not simply due to habitual excessive salt or water intakes. RAAS hyperactivation associated with hypovolemia was often accompanied by orthostatic dizziness or transient limb weakness, and edema was presumed to occur when the patient attempted to compensate for such situations by taking slightly excessive amounts of salt and water. During hospitalization, transient edema and mild elevation of brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) were observed after isotonic saline infusion, but both resolved spontaneously within several days as RAAS activity normalized. At discharge, the patient was instructed to continue potassium supplementation and to maintain adequate salt and water intake. While adherence was good, her electrolyte balance remained stable, and no recurrence of edema was observed. However, following a lapse in clinic visits during warm weather, she ran out of medication and experienced recurrence of hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis. This clinical course reinforces the importance of sustained treatment adherence and volume maintenance in preventing relapse of pseudo-BS. Figure 2. Discussion Pseudo-Bartter or Gitelman syndrome (pseudo-BS/GS) is a diagnostically challenging but clinically important entity that mimics inherited tubulopathies. While most cases of hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis are attributable to gastrointestinal losses, diuretic abuse, or eating disorders ( 4 ), a subset of patients lacks such antecedents, prompting consideration of acquired mimics of genetic disorders, such as BS/GS ( 5 , 6 ). Notably, up to 50% of adult patients clinically diagnosed with Gitelman syndrome do not harbor mutations in known causative genes, suggesting an alternative etiology ( 7 ). In our case, the patient exhibited persistent hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, elevated renin and aldosterone levels, and normotension, without diuretic use or overt volume loss. Imaging showed focal cortical atrophy of the right kidney with a reduced uptake on DMSA scintigraphy, suggestive of localized ischemia. Fluid therapy primarily corrected acute functional hypovolemia, thereby attenuating RAAS overactivity and improving markedly elevated renin activity observed at presentation. However, fluid therapy did not reverse the ischemic scarring. After discharge, when fluid intake was insufficient or during hot weather, the patient again demonstrated increased renin activity and disproportionate aldosterone responses. Thus, the pathophysiology appears to involve both systemic hypovolemia and focal renin activation within the scarred kidney, forming a vicious cycle in which volume depletion triggers further RAAS activation and clinical deterioration. Taken together, these findings suggest that the pathophysiology is acquired rather than genetic in nature. Although genetic testing was not performed, the diagnosis of true BS/GS was considered unlikely given the absence of a family history, reversibility of the biochemical abnormalities following isotonic saline infusion, and favorable clinical course. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that patients with BS/pseudo-BS exhibit blunted pressor responses to exogenous angiotensin II, attributed to chronic salt wasting, angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor desensitization, prostaglandin E 2 -mediated vasodilation, and hypokalemia-related vascular hyporeactivity ( 8 , 9 ). This phenomenon, together with systemic hypovolemia, may explain the paradox of marked RAAS activation in the absence of hypertension. While RAAS activation is classically attributed to systemic hypotension or volume depletion, local perfusion defects, such as those caused by infection-related renal scarring, may also independently trigger renin release and activate RAAS. Such focal renin activation, despite a preserved global renal function, has been reported in pediatric patients with urinary tract disease-associated renal scars, where renin-producing cells were enriched in fibrotic areas ( 10 ). Experimental studies further demonstrate that under homeostatic stress, such as a low sodium intake, hypotension, or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor treatment, vascular smooth muscle cells and other renin-lineage descendants can de-differentiate and re-acquire renin-producing capacity ( 11 ), suggesting that chronic renal injury or fibrosis may promote focal renin release and reactivation via cellular reprogramming. In our case, focal renal ischemia likely served as a trigger for RAAS activation, while chronic undernutrition may have amplified its downstream effects, ultimately leading to potassium-wasting alkalosis and a biochemical profile resembling BS/GS. Another noteworthy observation was the exaggerated aldosterone response during furosemide loading, which appeared disproportionate to the modest increase in PRA. This finding suggests a state of heightened adrenal sensitivity to angiotensin II (Ang II). Previous studies have shown that chronic sodium depletion can upregulate AT1 receptors in the adrenal zona glomerulosa, thereby augmenting aldosterone responsiveness ( 12 , 13 ). In addition, chronic activation of the RAAS itself may also induce structural and functional remodeling of the adrenal cortex, leading to a hyper-responsive state. Prolonged Ang II stimulation has been shown to enhance aldosterone synthesis not only by upregulating CYP11B2 transcription but also by increasing cholesterol uptake from circulating lipoproteins, augmenting StAR protein expression, and enlarging zona glomerulosa cells. Furthermore, chronic Ang II exposure increases the expression of AT1 receptors in glomerulosa cells, thereby amplifying adrenal responsiveness to circulating stimuli ( 14 ). In our case, sustained Ang II stimulation resulting from chronic sodium deficiency may have promoted structural and functional remodeling of the adrenal zona glomerulosa, thereby inducing excessive aldosterone secretion and diminished responsiveness to potassium supplementation, despite normotension. Nutritional and hormonal parameters further support the presence of chronic catabolic stress. The patient exhibited a low BMI and persistently reduced IGF-1 levels, vitamin D deficiency, and secondary hyperparathyroidism. A multicenter retrospective study from Japan reported that patients with pseudo-GS presented with a significantly lower BMI than genetically confirmed GS cases (18.7±4.8 vs. 21.2±3.5 kg/m 2 , p=0.02), along with an older age at the diagnosis, female predominance, and lower eGFR ( 15 ). The marked reduction in the BMI in pseudo-GS is thought to reflect the underlying chronic volume depletion and malnutrition, both of which may contribute to enhanced RAAS activity. In the present case, transient hyperuricemia that normalized rapidly after saline infusion further supports the presence of functional hypovolemia, distinguishing the presentation from true BS, in which serum urate typically remains unchanged despite the correction of metabolic abnormalities ( 16 ). Catabolic stress and prolonged electrolyte imbalance can also affect skeletal muscle function. In our patient, although chronic hypokalemic myopathy could not be completely excluded, the absence of persistent weakness and normal creatine kinase levels argued against structural muscle damage, suggesting that the functional effects of hypokalemia were the main contributors to her muscle symptoms. Importantly, in the present case, fluid and salt restriction in response to mild edema paradoxically exacerbated RAAS activation, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis. This represents a key clinical pitfall of pseudo-BS/GS, in which conventional volume restriction may be counterproductive by reinforcing hypovolemic stimuli. Notably, even a small amount of isotonic saline administered after the furosemide loading test resulted in transient peripheral edema and elevated BNP levels, suggesting persistent aldosterone excess and impaired sodium handling. A similar physiological mechanism has been described in patients with eating disorders, in whom delayed suppression of RAAS activity after the cessation of purging can lead to fluid retention and acute weight gain ( 17 ). This shared pathophysiological basis may also underlie the heightened fluid sensitivity observed in our case. In clinical practice, the presence of mild edema in such patients may reflect a transitional phase of recovery from RAAS hyperactivation, rather than simple overconsumption of salt and water. At this stage, maintaining an appropriate intake rather than imposing restrictions can stabilize the electrolyte balance without recurrence of edema, as shown by our patient's course. This consideration is particularly relevant when hypovolemia and compensatory behaviors coexist. This case highlights several important clinical and conceptual implications. First, in patients presenting with RAAS-driven hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for acquired mimics of hereditary tubulopathies, particularly in the absence of diuretic use, gastrointestinal loss, or genetic confirmation. Nutritional and imaging assessments may uncover overlooked contributors such as renal scarring, undernutrition, or chronic catabolic stress. Radiographic imaging with DMSA has long been recognized as a sensitive modality for detecting focal cortical scarring, particularly in patients with a history of recurrent urinary tract infections ( 18 ). Second, this case invites a re-examination of the current disease framework that dichotomizes BS/GS into strictly genetic versus acquired categories. Rather than being a binary distinction, these syndromes may exist on a functional spectrum of RAAS dysregulation, ranging from irreversible genetic defects to potentially reversible acquired states mediated by ischemia, inflammation, or nutritional factors. Recognizing this pathophysiological continuum may refine diagnostic accuracy and expand therapeutic approaches. Third, the limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Genetic testing was not performed, and histopathological confirmation of focal renin activation within the scarred kidney was unavailable. Nevertheless, the diagnosis was inferred based on the clinical presentation, biochemical reversibility, and imaging findings. Rapid improvement with fluid and salt repletion strongly supports the proposed mechanism. In conclusion, this case underscores the need for increased awareness of pseudo-BS/GS, particularly in cases driven by focal renal ischemia and nutritional stress. In patients with unexplained hypokalemic alkalosis, especially in women with a low BMI and a history of recurrent UTIs, clinicians should carefully consider focal renin activation within the scarred kidney as a potential contributor. Equally important, clinicians should avoid undue suspicion of diuretic abuse or eating disorders when encountering such patients because premature attribution may hinder an accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

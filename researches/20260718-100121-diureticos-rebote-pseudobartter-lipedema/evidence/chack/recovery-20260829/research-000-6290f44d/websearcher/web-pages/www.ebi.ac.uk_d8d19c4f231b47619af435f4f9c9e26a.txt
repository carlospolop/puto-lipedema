We included clinical studies reporting on outcomes such as mortality, heart failure recurrence, tolerability and feasibility. We assessed risk of bias and certainty of the evidence using the GRADE framework. We included 25 publications from 22 primary studies (15 randomized controlled trials; 7 nonrandomized studies). The mean number of participants in the deprescribing groups was 35, and median/mean age 64 years. In patients with heart failure, there was no clear evidence that diuretic deprescribing was associated with increased mortality compared to diuretic continuation (low certainty evidence). The risk of cardiovascular composite outcomes associated with diuretic deprescribing was inconsistent (studies showing lower risk for diuretic deprescribing, or comparable risk with diuretic continuation; very low certainty evidence). The effect on heart failure recurrence after diuretic deprescribing in patients with diuretics for heart failure, and of hypertension in patients with diuretics for hypertension was inconsistent across the included studies (low certainty evidence). In patients with diuretics for hypertension, diuretic deprescribing was well tolerated (moderate certainty evidence), while in patients with diuretics for heart failure, deprescribing diuretics can result in complaints of peripheral oedema (very low certainty evidence). The overall risk of bias was generally high. In summary, this systematic review suggests that diuretic discontinuation could be a safe and feasible treatment option for carefully selected patients. However, there isa lack of high‐quality evidence on its feasibility, safety and tolerability of diuretic deprescribing, warranting further research.</p><sec id="kwd-group1" sec-type="kwd-group" disp-level="2"><p><bold>Keywords:</bold> deprescribing, diuretics, heart failure, hypertension, patient‐relevant outcomes, systematic review</p></sec></abstract><trans-abstract id="trans-abstract1" xml:lang="nl" abstract-type="graphical"><title>Abstract</title>

<sup>1</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0002" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>2</sup>
</xref> Despite their established efficacy in treating acute decompensated HF, chronic and prolonged diuretic use in the absence of fluid retention is not supported by high‐quality evidence, and is associated with potentially life‐threatening adverse events,<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0003" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>3</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>4</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>5</sup>
</xref> especially in older patients.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0006" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>6</sup>
</xref> Notably, diuretics consistently rank among the drug classes with the highest rates of medication‐related hospital admissions.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0007" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>7</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0008" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>8</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0009" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>9</sup>
</xref> Continuation of cardiovascular medications when no longer necessary or posing greater potential risks <italic>vs</italic>. benefits may adversely affect quality of life (QoL), induce adverse drug reactions (ADRs), escalate healthcare costs and increase mortality.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0010" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>10</sup>
</xref> Further, chronic diuretic use may impede the guideline‐directed up‐titration of evidence‐based medications<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0011" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>11</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0012" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>12</sup>
</xref> and contribute to neurohumoral hyperactivation, particularly in older patients living with frailty.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0013" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>13</sup>
</xref> Observational data suggest that prognosis in HF patients without chronically using a loop diuretic is generally good and comparable to those receiving low or standard doses of diuretics.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0013" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>13</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0014" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>14</sup>
</xref> As such, reassessment of the benefits and harms of diuretics is good practice, and may bring up consideration of dose reduction or stopping of diuretics (deprescribing).</p><p>Since 2016, the European Society of Cardiology guidelines,<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0015" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>15</sup>
</xref> advocate for the de‐escalation of diuretics in stable HF patients. In addition, deprescribing antihypertensives is recommended in older and frail individuals with symptomatic low blood pressure (BP).<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0016" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>16</sup>
</xref> Despite these recommendations, diuretics are frequently overused and excessively dosed.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0017" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>17</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0018" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>18</sup>
</xref> However, there are good reasons to deprescribe diuretics; for example, if there is no valid indication (anymore) for diuretic use, or if the diuretic is prescribed as part of a prescribing cascade.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0019" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>19</sup>
</xref> In these settings, deprescribing should be considered. In addition, diuretic deprescribing may be appropriate if alternatives with more favourable benefit/risk ratios are available. The imperative to reassess the appropriateness of prolonged diuretic therapy and contemplate diuretic deprescribing is particularly pertinent in the context of HF, given the advent of innovative drugs such as sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT‐2) inhibitors and angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0020" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>20</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0021" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>21</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0022" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>22</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0023" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>23</sup>
</xref>
</p><p>In routine practice, however, deprescribing remains underutilized, as evidenced by the limited implementation of guideline‐recommended loop diuretic dose reductions in specialized HF clinics across 33 European and European Society of Cardiology‐affiliated countries.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0024" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>24</sup>
</xref> Various barriers to deprescribing related to patients, healthcare providers and healthcare systems have been identified,<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0025" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>25</sup>
</xref> which includes uncertainties in the benefits and harms of stopping or reducing medications compared to continuing. Guidelines and tools have been proposed as strategies to increase uptake of deprescribing in practice. To support healthcare providers in judicious decision‐making regarding when and how to safely taper or withdraw diuretics, our research group is presently developing an evidence‐based diuretic deprescribing clinical practice guideline. To inform the guideline, we conducted a systematic review to assess the advantages and drawbacks of diuretic deprescribing in comparison to the continued use of diuretics, and the feasibility of diuretic deprescribing in clinical practice.</p></sec><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0002" disp-level="1"><label>2.</label><title>METHODS</title><p>This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO, the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42022371658) and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses‐network meta‐analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0026" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>26</sup>
</xref> (Table <xref rid="bcp16189-supitem-0001" ref-type="supplementary-material">S1</xref>).</p><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0003" disp-level="2"><label>2.1.</label><title>Literature search</title><p>The search strategy was developed in consultation with an experienced medical librarian. We searched Ovid Medline, Embase and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (via Cochrane Library). Key concepts were (i) diuretics and (ii) deprescribing. The search covered studies from inception of each database until 16 December 2022. We updated the search on 28 May 2024. We employed a backward snowballing approach from the reference lists of the included publications to identify potential additional relevant studies. The initial search strategy, and the search update are presented in Tables <xref rid="bcp16189-supitem-0001" ref-type="supplementary-material">S2 and S3</xref>.</p><p>We imported the references identified through the search into EndNote X9 reference management software (Clarivate) and deduplicated them. Next, we exported the references to the Covidence program to facilitate the next steps in the systematic review process (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia. Available at <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://www.covidence.org" ext-link-type="uri">www.covidence.org</ext-link>).</p></sec><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0004" disp-level="2"><label>2.2.</label><title>Criteria for considering studies for the review</title><p>We considered studies for inclusion if they reported effects of a diuretic deprescribing intervention, irrespective of study population, reasons for diuretic use or study follow‐up duration. We excluded studies published in non‐English language and studies reporting on deprescribing diuretic in temporary and/or acute conditions (e.g., perioperatively). Our outcomes of interest included, but were not limited to all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, cardiovascular events (e.g., myocardial infarction, stroke), adverse diuretic withdrawal events (e.g., recurrence of HF or HT), health‐related QoL, and improvement/resolution of medication side effects or pill burden after diuretic withdrawal. The 2 independent reviewers discussed their conflicts until reaching consensus. When consensus was not reached, a third reviewer (N.v.d.V.) was involved in the discussion.</p></sec><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0007" disp-level="3"><label>2.3.2.</label><title>Data collection and management</title><p>Relevant data from the included publications were extracted into a data collection form by 2 independent reviewers (E.v.P., L.P., E.P., S.Ö., G.B.). Data collection included information on the study characteristics, diuretic deprescribing approach, primary and secondary outcomes. The 2 independent reviewers discussed their conflicts until reaching consensus. When consensus was not reached, a third reviewer (N.v.d.V.) was involved in the discussion.</p></sec><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0008" disp-level="3"><label>2.3.3.</label><title>Data synthesis and measures of the treatment effect</title><p>We focused on outcomes that we deemed are most important to patients, such as HF‐related hospital admissions, all‐cause mortality and QoL. Due to considerable heterogeneity in study design, patient characteristics, deprescribing interventions and/or outcomes reporting, it was not appropriate to combine the studies into a meta‐analysis. We narratively summarized the data by grouping outcome data based on patient population/condition: (i) adults taking diuretics for HF; (ii) adults taking diuretics for HT; and (iii) adults taking diuretics for other (e.g., peripheral oedema) or not specified indications. We did not perform subgroup or sensitivity analyses due to the limited number of studies for each outcome.</p><p>In addition, we narratively summarized the effects of diuretic deprescribing on other clinical outcomes (including effects on postprandial hypotension), biochemistry and cardiac ultrasonography measurements, and on the success‐rate of diuretic deprescribing attempts. Moreover, we narratively summarized the patient characteristics associated with successful diuretic deprescribing attempts.</p></sec><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0009" disp-level="3"><label>2.3.4.</label><title>Risk of bias assessment of included studies and certainty of evidence</title><p>The assessment of the quality of eligible studies was conducted by 2 independent reviewers (W.T., L.M., V.V.) using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) tool<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0027" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>27</sup>
</xref> for randomized trials, and the Risk Of Bias In Non‐Randomized Studies—of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0028" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>28</sup>
</xref> for nonrandomized trials and observational studies having a control group. Each potential source of bias was ranked as high, low or unclear, with quotes provided from the study to justify the bias grade. We used RoB only as a descriptive measure (we did not exclude eligible studies based on the RoB assessment). Disagreements in scoring were resolved by discussion between the 2 independent reviewers, and further discussed with a third independent reviewer (E.v.P.) to reach consensus. We visualized the results using the RoBVis web application (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://github.com/mcguinlu/robvis" ext-link-type="uri">https://github.com/mcguinlu/robvis</ext-link>). We assessed certainty of the evidence based on guidance provided in the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations) handbook (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://gdt.gradepro.org/app/handbook/handbook.html" ext-link-type="uri">https://gdt.gradepro.org/app/handbook/handbook.html</ext-link>) and an evidence‐based medicine primer article.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0029" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>29</sup>
</xref>
</p></sec></sec></sec><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0010" disp-level="1"><label>3.</label><title>RESULTS</title><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0011" disp-level="2"><label>3.1.</label><title>Description of studies</title><p>A total of 7449 unique records were retrieved from databases and other sources (PRISMA flow‐chart, Figure <xref rid="bcp16189-fig-0001" ref-type="fig">1</xref>). We reviewed 78 full text articles for eligibility. We included 25 reports from 22 studies. The details of the included studies are summarized in Table <xref rid="bcp16189-supitem-0001" ref-type="supplementary-material">S4</xref>. The studies were published between 1977 and 2023. Deprescribing approaches included complete withdrawal and/or dose reduction. Fourteen trials were conducted in Europe, 6 in the Americas (North America, South America, Central America, Caribbean) and 2 in Asia Pacific (Central and South Asia, Northeastern Asia, Southeastern Asia, Australia, and Oceania). Fifteen studies were randomized controlled trials; 7 were nonrandomized studies. Study follow‐up duration ranged from 2 weeks to 6 years. Most studies (10/22 = 45%) included participants with HF, 27% (6/22) with HT, 9% (2/22) peripheral oedema and 18% (4/22) included participants with diuretics for HF, HT, peripheral oedema and/or unknown reasons. The mean number of participants in the diuretic deprescribing groups was 35 (range 13–671). The median/mean age of the participants in the diuretic deprescribing groups was 64 years (range 56–82 years), and the median percentage of females was 36% (range 0–88%). Our findings underscore the paucity of high‐quality evidence on the feasibility, safety and tolerability of diuretic deprescribing: most studies included in this review exhibited limitations such as small sample sizes, short follow‐up durations and overall low to moderate quality, with a heightened susceptibility to bias. Heterogeneity among the trials was substantial. Although based on data from a limited set of clinical trials with a low grade of evidence, we found that diuretic therapy discontinuation could be a safe and feasible treatment option that deserves consideration for carefully selected patients. In patients with HF, mortality risk associated with diuretic deprescribing was comparable to the risk with diuretic continuation (low certainty evidence). The risk of cardiovascular composite outcomes including mortality associated with diuretic deprescribing was inconsistent, with studies showing lower risk for diuretic deprescribing, or comparable with diuretic continuation (very low certainty evidence). The effect on recurrence of HF after diuretic deprescribing in patients with diuretics for HF, and of HT in patients with diuretics for HT was inconsistent across the included studies (low certainty evidence). In patients with diuretics for HT, diuretic deprescribing was well tolerated (moderate certainty evidence), while in patients with diuretics for HF, deprescribing diuretics can result in complaints of peripheral oedema (very low certainty evidence). In patients with diuretics for HT, however, the risk of developing peripheral oedema was comparable in the diuretic deprescribing compared to the continuation group (moderate quality evidence).</p><p>Across the studies included in this review, the feasibility of diuretic deprescribing varied considerably with success percentages ranging from 5 to 100%. Patient characteristics associated with successful deprescribing included the absence of valid diuretic indications or a history/diagnosis of HF, and left ventricular ejection fraction deprescribing failures were mostly attributed to (recurrent) congestive HF episodes or HT recurrence. Mortality outcomes in HF trials showed inconsistent results, which is in line with the findings of several reviews on the effects of deprescribing of medications.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0054" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>54</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0055" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>55</sup>
</xref> Data from the included studies suggested that diuretic deprescribing compared to diuretic continuation improves renal function. Biochemical (cardiometabolic and neurohumoral biomarkers) and cardiac ultrasonography measurements were comparable between diuretic deprescribing and continuation groups. Tolerability was generally acceptable, but the available evidence pertaining to the tolerability and effects on well‐being or QoL associated with diuretic deprescribing was notably limited. This discrepancy is noteworthy given that deprescribing inherently embodies a patient‐centred approach, with the explicit goal of optimizing pharmacotherapy to enhance patient outcomes.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0056" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>56</sup>
</xref>
</p><p>There has been a limited number of prior systematic reviews investigating the safety and feasibility of antihypertensive withdrawal including diuretics. One such review, encompassing 66 publications, indicated that approximately 25% of individuals using antihypertensives could safely discontinue the medication without experiencing a recurrence of HT.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0057" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>57</sup>
</xref> Notably, systematic reviews focusing on older adults observed an increase in systolic and diastolic BP following withdrawal, but no statistically significant difference in mortality.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0054" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>54</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0058" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>58</sup>
</xref>
</p><p>The evidence derived from the data included in our review has several limitations. First, we judged the certainty of the evidence to be low or very low for all outcomes considered. Therefore, we are uncertain in the overall evidence on diuretic deprescribing. Methodological and reporting limitations were prevalent across the majority of included studies, and unexpected observations in randomized studies raised concerns about small study effects, selection bias and publication bias. Because there were too few studies to establish asymmetry visually in funnel plots, we were not able to assess publication bias. We downgraded certainty primarily due to the RoB related to imprecision, inconsistency and indirectness. Furthermore, it is challenging to determine the applicability of our findings to contemporary clinical practice: the majority of the HF trials were performed decades ago in relatively healthy, young and predominantly male HFrEF patients. In contrast, contemporary real‐world HF populations are characterized by patients who are predominantly older, frail and multimorbid,<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0059" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>59</sup>
</xref> with a large proportion of female patients with HFpEF.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0060" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>60</sup>
</xref> However, none of the trials included in our review measured or reported frailty, and only 1 nonrandomized study was performed in a nursing home population,<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0031" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>31</sup>
</xref> with residents who were likely to be frail. Frailty is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes and ADRs in older HF patients.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0061" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>61</sup>
</xref> The paucity of studies underscores the exclusion of vulnerable populations from HF clinical trials.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0061" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>61</sup>
</xref> The association between frailty and adverse outcomes, coupled with the increased risk of ADRs in older HF patients, underscores the importance of considering frailty status in diuretic deprescribing decision‐making.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0013" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>13</sup>
</xref> Based on their increased risk of both acute decompensated HF episodes and adverse drug events in pre‐frail and frail individuals (Clinical Frailty Scale ≥ 4), the decision to deprescribe should be weighed carefully and repeatedly over time, and with greater vigilance compared to individuals with a lower Clinical Frailty Scale score. In addition, it should be emphasized that HF standards of care at time of the majority of studies were of notoriously lower quality compared to contemporary care, characterized by evidence‐based disease‐modifying drugs and multidisciplinary HF outpatient programmes.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0062" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>62</sup>
</xref> Literature suggests that patients treated for HF according to the current guidelines are less likely to develop HF after deprescribing diuretics.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0063" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>63</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0064" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>64</sup>
</xref>
</p><p>There are limitations to our review process. First, the term <italic>deprescribing</italic> is relatively new,<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0065" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>65</sup>
</xref> even though the concept and clinical practice have existed for a long time. Additionally, given the rapid evolution in the field, we may have missed potentially relevant studies. By developing a comprehensive literature search by an experienced medical librarian and using a variety of different search terms for deprescribing/withdrawal, however, we mitigated this risk. Second, it is important to consider that we excluded observational studies reporting on the effects of diuretic deprescribing in individuals both before and after deprescribing. Data from these observations may be valuable for real‐world clinical practice. By contrast, the scope of our review was broad (we did not exclude based on the indication for diuretic use), aligning with real‐life clinical practice needs. Third, we excluded studies in which diuretics were deprescribed after initiating a medication from another drug class, for example SGLT‐2 inhibitors. In the currently rapidly evolving landscape of HF treatment, with the newer medication classes such as angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors and SGLT‐2 inhibitors, this is a shortcoming. In addition, we grouped the studies based on the indication for diuretic use (HF, HT and peripheral oedema). The reason for this approach was pragmatic, and to facilitate data synthesis. However, we realize that it is likely that 1 or more indicator conditions occurred concomitantly in individuals included in the trials. The strengths of our systematic review process include the methodological rigour and the comprehensive literature search. Search limitations of animal and non‐English studies were performed manually rather than relying on automatic limitations to reduce retrieval loss.</p><p>This systematic review has several implications for clinical practice and future research. With regard to clinical practice, we intend to incorporate the findings of this review into an evidence‐based diuretic deprescribing clinical practice guideline that we are currently developing. While recognizing the imperfect nature of the available evidence from this systematic review, our findings compile the best available evidence on the clinical effects of diuretic deprescribing. With this clinical practice guideline, we aim to support healthcare providers in judicious decision‐making regarding when and how to safely taper or withdraw diuretics. In terms of future research, our review underscores the need for well‐designed clinical trials targeting representative contemporary patient populations. The scarcity of literature on diuretic deprescribing in older, frail individuals warrants future studies incorporating frailty subgroups. Defining a core outcome set for diuretic deprescribing studies, including frailty assessment and relevant outcomes such as falls, urinary incontinence, and functionality will enhance result synthesis and contribute to a more substantial body of evidence. Emphasis on patient‐centred outcomes, including preferences for communication about diuretic deprescribing proposals and changes in health‐related QoL, is crucial.<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0066" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>66</sup>
</xref> Moreover, investigations into the relationship between diuretic deprescribing and economic outcomes are needed.</p></sec><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0020" disp-level="1"><label>5.</label><title>CONCLUSIONS</title><p>In conclusion, our systematic review provides valuable insights into the challenges and opportunities associated with diuretic deprescribing. Although based on data from a limited set of clinical trials with a low grade of evidence, we conclude that diuretic therapy discontinuation could be a safe and feasible treatment option that deserves consideration for carefully selected patients: successful deprescribing attempts were associated with specific patient characteristics. Additionally, we demonstrated that the benefit/risk balance of diuretic deprescribing compared to continued use may vary dependent on the clinical setting. For example, the risk of recurrent HF was comparable between diuretic deprescribing and diuretic continuation in HF patients, whereas the risk was higher patients with HT who discontinued diuretics. This underscores the need for individualized diuretic deprescribing approaches. Nevertheless, there is a paucity of high‐certainty evidence applicable to current practice, and particularly in situations where deprescribing is often considered clinically, such as frailty and falls. There is also a lack of data on key outcomes crucial to older adults, including functional independence,<xref rid="bcp16189-bib-0067" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>67</sup>
</xref> falls and QoL. Future research efforts are crucial to address these knowledge gaps, particularly in understanding the safety and potential harms of diuretic deprescribing in older and multimorbid adults. Standardization in future diuretic deprescribing interventions and consistent reporting of outcomes in relevant patient categories are essential to enhance our understanding and refine diuretic deprescribing in clinical practice.</p><p>In the meanwhile, the insights from this systematic review represent the best available evidence on clinical experience with diuretic deprescribing. As such, our findings will contribute to the formulation of diuretic deprescribing recommendations within the evidence‐based diuretic deprescribing clinical practice guideline currently under development by our group.</p></sec><sec id="bcp16189-sec-0021" disp-level="1"><title>AUTHOR CONTRIBUTIONS</title><p>Eveline van Poelgeest was involved in the conception and design of the study, acquisition of data, data analysis and interpretation, drafting and revising the article.

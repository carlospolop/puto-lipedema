PubMed/NCBI records, Europe PMC full text, Crossref DOI metadata, NCBI Bookshelf guidance, DailyMed labels, and accessible primary PDFs/text were searched and inspected. Full-text and raw API artifacts were preserved in the requested directory. Direct human/animal studies, mechanistic experiments, clinical guidance, reviews, anecdotes, and marketing-related evidence are separated below. No conclusion that lipedema causes this syndrome is made.\n\nOVERALL CONCLUSION\nAcquired pseudo-Bartter-like presentation is a biochemical phenotype: hypokalemia, hypochloremia and metabolic alkalosis, usually with secondary hyperreninemic hyperaldosteronism and low or normal blood pressure. It is generated by either renal chloride/sodium loss or extrarenal chloride/volume loss, then maintained by effective arterial-volume contraction, reduced GFR, chloride deficiency, potassium deficiency, aldosterone, increased distal sodium delivery, and impaired bicarbonate secretion. Edema does not prove adequate effective arterial volume: third-spacing states can retain total-body sodium while reducing arterial filling. Conversely, visible leg enlargement in lipedema is not evidence of RAAS activation or a diuretic-responsive syndrome.\n\nPHYSIOLOGY\n1. Effective arterial volume. Reduced arterial filling lowers renal perfusion and activates renin, angiotensin II, and aldosterone. Angiotensin II increases proximal sodium and bicarbonate reabsorption; aldosterone increases epithelial sodium-channel activity in the collecting duct and stimulates potassium and hydrogen secretion. If a loop or thiazide is still active, sodium and chloride delivery to distal nephron segments is increased, amplifying this process.\n\n2. Loop and thiazide exposure. Loop agents inhibit NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb; thiazides inhibit NCC in the distal convoluted tubule. Both cause NaCl loss, but loops usually produce a stronger distal sodium-delivery and alkalosis signal. ENaC-mediated sodium uptake makes the lumen more negative, favoring ROMK potassium secretion and hydrogen secretion through H+-ATPase and H+-K+-ATPase pathways. Aldosterone magnifies these effects.\n\n3. Chloride depletion. Vomiting or nasogastric suction directly removes gastric HCl, simultaneously losing hydrogen and chloride and adding retained bicarbonate to extracellular fluid. Chloride depletion also reduces renal bicarbonate secretion, including through the pendrin Cl−/HCO3− exchanger in beta-intercalated cells. Volume contraction lowers GFR and increases proximal bicarbonate reabsorption, maintaining alkalosis after the original loss has stopped.\n\n4. H+/K+ shifts and ammoniagenesis. Alkalemia shifts potassium intracellularly, but a transcellular shift is not the same as total-body potassium depletion. Sustained potassium deficiency promotes intracellular hydrogen movement, renal hydrogen secretion, bicarbonate reabsorption, and ammonium excretion. In the kidney, glutamine metabolism produces NH3; NH4+ is secreted, recycled, and trapped in the medulla. Net ammonium excretion removes acid and generates new bicarbonate. Goodman, Fuisz, and Cahill studied renal cortical slices from acid-, alkali-, and potassium-depleted rats and found increased gluconeogenesis from glutamine/glutamate/alpha-ketoglutarate linked to increased ammonia production. This is animal mechanistic evidence, not a clinical assay.\n\n5. Important counterevidence. Huth, Squires, and Elkinton performed four balance studies in three normal men subjected to dietary potassium depletion, with later desoxycorticosterone exposure. Potassium deficit increased urinary ammonium and alkalosis varied from slight to substantial; potassium depletion alone did not reproduce the uniform severe alkalosis seen in clinical volume/chloride-depletion states. Thus, a simple potassium-shift explanation is incomplete.\n\nURINE CHLORIDE AND URINE POTASSIUM BY TIMING\nThe Core Curriculum 2022 algorithm uses urine chloride below approximately 20 mmol/L as supportive of chloride-responsive contraction and values above that as supportive of ongoing renal chloride loss, mineralocorticoid effect, or active diuretic exposure. Older vomiting literature used a 24-hour chloride below 10 mEq/L versus above 20 mEq/L in Bartter syndrome. These are clinical interpretation cut-points, not home treatment thresholds.\n\nA low urine chloride is expected when kidneys are conserving chloride after vomiting, many laxative/intestinal losses, or sweat/heat losses. A high value is expected with active renal chloride wasting, recent loop/thiazide exposure, or mineralocorticoid-mediated sodium retention. A recent diuretic dose can therefore make an extrarenal-loss patient look renal; after the diuretic effect has waned, severe volume contraction can make urine chloride fall. Saline or chloride administration, ongoing intermittent dosing, potassium replacement, dilution, chronic kidney disease, and mixed losses further complicate interpretation. Cystic fibrosis/pseudo-Bartter is a direct counterexample: urine chloride may be low or high depending on the renal and sweat-loss context.\n\nThe same timing problem affects urine potassium. Common tertiary clinical cut-points are 24-hour urine potassium above about 30 mEq/day, spot urine potassium above about 15 mEq/L, or a potassium/creatinine ratio above about 13 mEq/mmol as evidence of inappropriate renal potassium loss. These values are affected by urine concentration, collection timing, intake, replacement, kidney function, and low muscle mass. A low urine potassium can reflect appropriate renal conservation after severe depletion and does not exclude recent renal wasting. A high value can reflect active diuretic effect or secondary aldosterone after an extrarenal loss. Serial samples and exposure timing are more informative than one spot result.\n\nThe 1992 comparative series by Pisano et al. (PMID 1328936; DOI 10.1093/ndt/7.9.896) compared five surreptitious-diuretic pseudo-Bartter patients with six true Bartter patients. Blood sodium, chloride, potassium, bicarbonate, renin, and aldosterone overlapped. Daily and fasting urine sodium/chloride/potassium were generally lower in pseudo-Bartter but substantially overlapped. A furosemide challenge differed between groups, but the sample was tiny and old; no sensitivity or specificity was calculated. The result directly supports the limitation of electrolyte patterns alone.\n\nDIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS\n- Vomiting or NG suction: gastric HCl loss, volume contraction, secondary RAAS activation, low urine chloride once the kidney is conserving chloride, and hypokalemia from renal potassium wasting. Self-induced vomiting can closely mimic Bartter syndrome. A 1982 case report (PMID 7145783; DOI 10.1080/00325481.1982.11716299) described hypokalemic alkalosis and high renin/aldosterone from habitual vomiting; low 24-hour urine chloride was the distinguishing clue.\n- Laxatives and eating disorders: lower-GI bicarbonate loss often causes metabolic acidosis, but chronic purging, volume contraction, chloride loss, secondary aldosterone, poor intake, and mixed vomiting can produce alkalosis or an autonomous pseudo-Bartter-like state. A primary series of nine women aged 17–54 (PMID 2251604; no DOI indexed) documented hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, complications including weakness, paralysis, rhabdomyolysis, seizures and cardiac failure, and edema/weight gain after withdrawal. It is highly selected and old. In a separate primary study of 77 women with bulimia nervosa and 53 controls (PMID 11746287; DOI 10.1002/eat.1085), urine sodium/chloride ratio was the best discriminator but identified only 51.5% of BN subjects at a 5% false-positive rate; this is not a diuretic assay and is not pathognomonic.\n- Sweat and heat loss: salt and water loss activates secondary aldosterone and can produce hypokalemic alkalosis when chloride depletion and contraction are substantial. Cystic fibrosis is a clinically important acquired pseudo-Bartter mimic from sweat chloride loss; a 2024 pediatric case report (PMID 38296503; DOI 10.1136/bcr-2023-257348; PMC10831455) illustrates this presentation. The Core Curriculum notes that cystic fibrosis can produce either low or high urine chloride, so urine chloride is not a binary renal/extrarenal test.\n- Loop and thiazide diuretics: active drugs produce renal sodium/chloride and potassium loss; high urine chloride during active drug effect is not proof of Bartter syndrome. Labels for furosemide and hydrochlorothiazide explicitly warn about hypochloremic alkalosis, hypokalemia, volume depletion, renal impairment and arrhythmia risk.\n- Bartter and Gitelman syndromes: inherited salt-wasting disorders generally show persistent renal chloride loss, hyperreninemic hyperaldosteronism without hypertension, and characteristic calcium/magnesium patterns. Bartter tends toward hypercalciuria; Gitelman toward hypomagnesemia and hypocalciuria, but overlap is substantial. Bamgbola and Ahmed (PMID 33564404; DOI 10.1093/ckj/sfaa172; PMC7857843) emphasize that vomiting, laxatives, surreptitious diuretics, cystic fibrosis, congenital chloride diarrhea, Dent disease and other disorders can mimic them and that no single urine marker is diagnostic.\n- Adult and congenital mimics: a 2026 adult-onset Bartter case (PMID 41994147; DOI 10.1002/ccr3.72541) showed persistent hypokalemic alkalosis with high urine sodium, potassium and chloride after vomiting and sepsis, supporting a renal tubulopathy. A 2025 Dent disease case (PMID 40881168; DOI 10.1159/000543719) showed Bartter-like hypokalemic alkalosis, hypercalciuria, proteinuria and rickets. These are anecdotes, not prevalence estimates, but they show why genetic or tubular evaluation may be needed.\n- Mineralocorticoid excess: primary aldosteronism, cortisol-mediated mineralocorticoid activity, apparent mineralocorticoid excess/licorice, Liddle syndrome and related states generally cause renal potassium and hydrogen loss with hypertension or a masked blood-pressure phenotype. A primary case report of a 44-year-old woman with severe hypokalemia, alkalosis, hypomagnesemia and hypocalcemia (PMID 31498018; DOI 10.1080/0886022X.2019.1662439; PMC6746263) found an adrenal aldosterone-producing tumor despite only mild/controlled hypertension and a Gitelman-like biochemical picture. This directly argues against excluding aldosterone excess solely because blood pressure is not strikingly high.\n- Postsurgical and post-bariatric states: high-output ileostomy or chloride-rich diarrhea can produce low urine chloride, alkalosis, hypokalemia, dehydration and AKI. Bariatric anatomy can also impair absorption of oral potassium or other drugs, while vomiting, diarrhea, poor intake, DKA and insulin shifts create additional causes of hypokalemia.\n\nPOSTSURGICAL PRIMARY EVIDENCE\nAli et al. reported a 58-year-old man after hemicolectomy, distal ileal resection, ileostomy and colostomy (PMID 25874098; DOI 10.1093/ckj/sfs082; PMC4393485). He had approximately 3 L/day ostomy output, low urine sodium/chloride, chloride-rich stool, hypochloremic hypokalemic alkalosis, hypotension and acute renal failure. This is direct evidence for a postsurgical extrarenal chloride-loss mimic.\n\nKodra et al. described a woman after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass with persistent hypokalemia despite oral tablets, intact tablets in stool and improvement with powdered potassium (PMID 36213722; DOI 10.7759/cureus.28607; PMC9529024). Vomiting, diarrhea, dehydration and DKA were confounders, so this is a case-level demonstration that refractory hypokalemia after bariatric surgery may reflect absorption failure rather than renal kaliuresis.\n\nHirai et al. reported a 61-year-old man with gastric outlet stenosis from gastric cancer, recurrent vomiting, AKI, potassium 2.9 mEq/L, pH 7.66, bicarbonate 94 mmol/L and prolonged QTc; hydration and electrolyte correction improved renal function and alkalosis (PMID 33442435; PMC7788306). Provenance caveat: the PubMed record displayed DOI 10.1007/s10157-006-0435-6, whereas the PMC full text displays the article DOI 10.2185/jrm.2020-025; the latter is used here.\n\nLIPOEDEMA-SPECIFIC EVIDENCE\nThe targeted PubMed query combining lipedema/lipoedema with diuretic, hypokalemia, alkalosis or Bartter returned two records: a 2024 retrospective lipoedema liposuction cohort and a 2004 male lipoedema case. Europe PMC searches for lipoedema plus metabolic alkalosis, pseudo-Bartter or diuretic found no relevant full-text record. No lipedema-specific case report or cohort documenting acquired pseudo-Bartter syndrome or hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis after ordinary diuretic use, vomiting, sweat loss, laxatives or fluid depletion was found through the cutoff. This is an evidence gap, not proof of absence in practice.\n\nHoffmann et al. studied 47 conventional tumescent and 25 waterjet-assisted liposuction patients with lipoedema in a retrospective cohort (PMID 39476528; DOI 10.1016/j.bjps.2024.10.013). Postoperative serum potassium fell by 0.30 ± 0.24 mmol/L overall and 0.47 ± 0.31 mmol/L in the waterjet group; mean aspirate volumes were approximately 3.3–3.7 L. The study did not report metabolic alkalosis, urine chloride, urine potassium, renin, aldosterone, medication exposure, kidney outcomes, or causal lipedema biology. It is direct perioperative potassium evidence, not evidence that lipedema causes pseudo-Bartter.\n\nA 2004 male lipoedema case reported that weight reduction, compression and diuretic therapy were ineffective before liposuction (PMID 15006533; DOI 10.1016/j.bjps.2003.12.020). This is a single anecdote and supports neither efficacy nor a metabolic mechanism. As a non-lipedema comparator, five female volunteers undergoing large-volume ultrasound-assisted liposuction developed intraoperative hypokalemia and mild postoperative hyponatremia (PMID 15318060; DOI 10.1097/01.prs.0000131022.98481.6e). Fluid shifts and respiratory alkalosis were implicated; no lipedema or metabolic alkalosis was established. Applying pseudo-Bartter physiology to lipedema therefore remains extrapolation from exposure and surgery, not direct disease evidence.\n\nDIURETIC ASSAY PERFORMANCE, FALSE RESULTS AND TIMING\nThe primary 1992 GC-MS urine method (PMID 1430008; DOI 10.1016/0378-4347(92)80447-X) used direct extractive alkylation, SM-7 resin cleanup and 1 mL urine. Reported analytical detection limits were 10–50 ng/mL for a panel including furosemide, hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone, bumetanide and metolazone. These are limits of detection for an older method, not clinical sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value or a validated window of detection in pseudo-Bartter patients.\n\nRitscher et al. compared paired serum and urine LC-MS/MS in 26 unsupervised outpatients (PMID 32400211; DOI 10.1080/08037051.2020.1763775). The study included hydrochlorothiazide in 12 patients, furosemide in two and canrenone, a spironolactone metabolite, in six. Urine detectability classified 88% of prescriptions as adherent; serum dose-related cutoffs classified 81%, whereas literature therapeutic ranges classified 50%. The authors explicitly warned that high metabolism, low renal excretion and prolonged detectability can mislead interpretation. This is an adherence study, not a pseudo-Bartter validation study.\n\nLauder et al. studied 56 hypertension patients using LC-high-resolution MS in urine, plasma and oral fluid (PMID 32633188; DOI 10.1161/JAHA.119.014180; PMC7660713). Complete adherence by urine detection occurred in 59%, partial adherence in 29% and nonadherence in 13%; 181 drug detections occurred in urine versus 119 in plasma and 88 in oral fluid. Diuretics were detected less reliably in oral fluid than urine/plasma. It demonstrates matrix effects and feasibility, not disease-level sensitivity or specificity.\n\nA 2020 prospective study of 54 apparent refractory-hypertension patients used 24-hour urine HPLC-MS/MS (PMID 31813346; DOI 10.1161/HYPERTENSIONAHA.119.14137). Among 40 fully evaluated patients, 16 were fully adherent, 18 partially adherent and six had no prescribed agents detected. This shows that a negative urine result often reflects absent or intermittent exposure in the sampled period, but it does not establish whether a patient who took a drug outside the collection window would test negative.\n\nPharmacokinetic studies clarify why timing varies. Favretto et al. studied a contaminated supplement, one athlete and three healthy volunteers; LC-MS/MS found hydrochlorothiazide in post-ingestion urine at 5–230 ng/mL (PMID 30194914; DOI 10.1002/dta.2499). This supports inadvertent low-level positives. A two-volunteer five-day study of low-dose chlorthalidone and hydrochlorothiazide found rapid, higher urinary hydrochlorothiazide excretion but slower, lower-concentration chlorthalidone elimination, with corresponding urine concentrations of 45 and 53 ng/mL (PMID 38374541; DOI 10.1002/dta.3661). Both are small studies and do not provide a clinical collection schedule. The 2026 chlorthalidone hepatocyte study (PMID 40668262; DOI 10.1093/jat/bkaf072) identified candidate metabolites in vitro but did not establish clinical sensitivity.\n\nPractical interpretation: a positive parent drug or validated metabolite is evidence of exposure to that analyte, not proof of dose, intent, timing, or causal responsibility for alkalosis. A negative result means not detected in that specimen by that panel and method at that time. False negatives can arise from late collection, intermittent use, dilution, low renal excretion, metabolism to an untested analyte, low dose, incomplete panel coverage or assay limitations. False positives can arise from contamination, cross-reactivity or misinterpretation of persistent metabolites. No robust, standardized clinical sensitivity/specificity or universal timing window was identified for the pseudo-Bartter diagnostic setting.\n\nREBOUND EDEMA, NATURAL DIURETICS AND POTASSIUM-SPARING DRUGS\nThe eating-disorder review by Puckett (2023; PMID 36803805; DOI 10.1186/s40337-023-00751-w; PMC9942359) summarizes pseudo-Bartter edema and rapid weight gain after purging cessation, but it is a secondary review and its edema evidence is largely selected case reports. The primary 1990 laxative-abuse series documents a real edema/secondary-aldosterone phenomenon, but cannot establish that every post-withdrawal edema episode is rebound edema.\n\nCounterevidence comes from a primary 102-patient idiopathic-edema study (PMID 7894988; no DOI indexed). Among 91 symptomatic patients, urine contained a diuretic in 16 and a laxative in four; none had gross serum urea/electrolyte abnormalities, and many had no prior prescribed diuretic exposure or evidence of plasma-volume depletion. Thus, visible edema should not automatically be attributed to diuretic rebound, purging or a need for more diuresis.\n\nNo controlled clinical evidence identified here supports marketed natural diuretics as treatment for lipoedema or acquired pseudo-Bartter. The Favretto contamination study shows that products labeled as nutritional supplements can contain pharmacologic hydrochlorothiazide and generate measurable urinary exposure. A natural label is not evidence of electrolyte safety, drug identity, or efficacy.\n\nCurrent DailyMed labels are important counterevidence to automatic spironolactone or amiloride use. The amiloride label, effective 28 July 2026, says monotherapy should rarely be used and should be reserved for documented persistent hypokalemia with careful monitoring; it contraindicates anuria and significant renal insufficiency and warns about rapid, potentially fatal hyperkalemia, especially with renal disease, diabetes, potassium supplements, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, cyclosporine or tacrolimus. The spironolactone label, effective 17 August 2026, warns about hyperkalemia, salt depletion, renal deterioration, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, trimethoprim, potassium supplements and lithium; it also lists hypochloremic alkalosis among possible electrolyte effects. The label statement that spironolactone may help edema when another diuretic caused hypokalemia is a labeled context, not evidence for empiric treatment of unexplained lipedema swelling.\n\nFurosemide and hydrochlorothiazide labels, effective 17 August and 6 August 2026 respectively, warn about hypochloremic alkalosis, hypokalemia, dehydration, hypotension, oliguria, arrhythmia, renal deterioration and lithium interactions. They specifically emphasize serum and urine electrolyte assessment when vomiting or parenteral fluids are present. These labels support exposure review and clinician monitoring, not self-directed cycling or replacement protocols.\n\nURGENT CLINICAL WARNING SIGNS\nThe combination of severe weakness, paralysis, palpitations, syncope, ECG change, respiratory muscle weakness, confusion, seizure, marked drowsiness, hypotension, oliguria or acute kidney injury is potentially emergent. Ongoing vomiting, high-output diarrhea/ostomy, heat illness, inability to retain fluids or medication, or suspected diuretic/foreign-product exposure also warrants prompt clinical evaluation. No home thresholds or dosing protocols are provided here.\n\nSOURCE-BY-SOURCE EXTRACTED RECORD\n1. Do C, Vasquez PC, Soleimani M.

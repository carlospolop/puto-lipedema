# Diuretics in lipedema: evidence coverage map

Run/as-of date: 2026-08-29. Scope: Spanish patient-information audit; no patient-specific tapering or dose advice.

## A. Guideline and consensus anchors

### German S2k lipedema guideline
- Exact title: **S2k-Leitlinie Lipödem** (English PDF: *S2K Lipoedema Guidelines*), AWMF register 037-012, version 5.0.
- Issuing body: Deutsche Gesellschaft für Phlebologie und Lymphologie e.V. (DGPL), AWMF.
- AWMF record: https://register.awmf.org/de/leitlinien/detail/037-012 . AWMF API reports release 31.01.2024, last edit 22.01.2024, valid to 21.01.2029. Crossref DOI for the journal publication: https://doi.org/10.1111/ddg.15513 (no PMID located).
- Long German PDF: https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/037-012l_S2k_Lipoedem_2024-01_01.pdf . English PDF: https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/037_D_Ges_fuer_Phlebologie/037-012le_S2k_Lipoedema__2024-08.pdf . Spanish recommendations translation (posted 2025-02): https://register.awmf.org/assets/guidelines/037_D_Ges_fuer_Phlebologie/037-012emp-spanisch_S2k_Lipoedem_2025-02.pdf .
- Exact diuretic recommendation: “Diuretics shall not be used for treating lipoedema. Diuretics may be used for lipoedema patients based on internal indications.” Consensus 94.4% in the English text; the Spanish translation displays 94.1%.
- It separates lipedema from edema/lymphedema, while recognizing coincident obesity-associated lymphedema, venous disease, orthostatic congestion, and idiopathic edema. It reports small, heterogeneous lymphatic/imaging observations: slower or altered flow in some studies, but no diagnostic edema on ultrasound and “pure” lipedema MRI without edema; it cautions that some apparent lipo-lymphedema may be obesity-associated.
- It states that idiopathic edema/fluid-retention syndrome can co-occur, and long-term diuretic abuse may aggravate edema through reduced plasma volume, potassium loss, renal salt/water retention, and secondary aldosteronism. This is mechanistic consensus narrative, not a lipedema drug trial.
- S2k is structured consensus, not an S3 systematic-evidence guideline. Authors describe manual searches of PubMed, Web of Science, Research Net, and international guidelines; no fully reproducible systematic search or individual GRADE evidence table was located. Consensus conference: 18.10.2023, 18 voting participants; no public external consultation. No third-party funding; DGPL paid conference/limited travel costs, and COIs were disclosed and managed by recusal/double voting rules.

### US Standard of Care
- Herbst et al., **Standard of care for lipedema in the United States**, Phlebology 2021; DOI https://doi.org/10.1177/02683555211015887, PMID 34049453, PMCID PMC8652358, online 28.05.2021; OA: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8652358/ .
- Delphi consensus of 21 experts plus parliamentarian: 96 statements, 85 final; consensus threshold 75%. It says no medication specifically treats lipedema; “Long-term use of diuretics should be avoided in people with lipedema. (⊕B)” and immediately says diuretics do not treat the main cause of edema in lipedema, described there as inflammation. It separately advises avoiding medications that increase edema and thiazolidinediones; comorbid lymphedema/venous/obesity disease should be assessed under its own guidance.
- Its ⊕/A-B-C scheme is GRADE-like (grade of recommendation plus certainty), but the diuretic statement cites tissue/pathophysiology papers and an educational Starling-principle reference, not a lipedema diuretic RCT. Thus Grade B should not be read as proof of direct drug efficacy.
- Funding: NIH/NHLBI grant 1 R13 HL147503-01; acknowledgements include material support from the Lipedema Foundation and Fat Disorders Resource Society. The article discloses several author relationships, including research funding, speaking honoraria, and consulting fees.

### ISL consensus documents
- 2020: **The Diagnosis and Treatment of Peripheral Lymphedema: 2020 Consensus Document of the International Society of Lymphology**, Lymphology 53:3-19, online 02.06.2020, DOI https://doi.org/10.2458/lymph.4649, OA PDF https://journals.librarypublishing.arizona.edu/lymph/article/id/4649/download/pdf/ .
- 2023: **The Diagnosis and Treatment of Peripheral Lymphedema: 2023 Consensus Document of the International Society of Lymphology**, Lymphology 56:133-151, journal page online 28.08.2024 (issue dated 2023), DOI https://doi.org/10.2458/lymph.6372, OA PDF https://journals.librarypublishing.arizona.edu/lymph/article/id/6372/download/pdf/ .
- Both say evidence is heterogeneous with few rigorous randomized long-term trials and no satisfactory meta-analysis. Both say diuretics have limited initial-CDT use, should be reserved for specific comorbidities/complications, and long-term use is discouraged because benefit in peripheral lymphedema is marginal and fluid/electrolyte imbalance can occur. They mention selected use for ascites/hydrothorax, protein-losing enteropathy, palliative care, and sometimes short courses for malignant obstruction. These documents address lymphedema, not pure lipedema.

## B. Direct lipedema drug/trial coverage

ClinicalTrials.gov v2 exact searches on 2026-08-29: “lipedema diuretic”, “lipedema furosemide”, “lipedema hydrochlorothiazide”, “lipedema chlorthalidone”, “lipedema spironolactone”, “lipedema amiloride”, “lipedema acetazolamide”, “lipedema SGLT2”, “lipedema empagliflozin”, “lipedema dapagliflozin”, “lipedema herbal diuretic”, and “lipedema diuretic deprescribing” all returned studies=[] / no clinical trials found. Broader “lipedema edema drug” found NCT06180850, a completed observational case-control biorepository (not a drug trial); “lipedema edema treatment” returned non-drug compression/physiotherapy studies, including NCT04492046.

PubMed E-utilities exact searches with lipedema/lipoedema plus each requested drug/class found zero for furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide, hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone, spironolactone, amiloride, acetazolamide, SGLT2, empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, herbal diuretic, deprescribing, withdrawal, and dependence. The broad diuretic query returned only PMID 36970766 (skin sodium scoping review) and PMID 15006533 (2004 male case report); neither is a drug trial. Europe PMC full-text title searches likewise found no direct drug trial. OpenAlex was used for discovery; Google Scholar was unavailable (no usable key) and Semantic Scholar was rate-limited on the final repeat.

Relevant low-level direct/near-direct evidence:
- Shin et al., **Lipedema, a Rare Disease**, Ann Rehabil Med 2011;35:922-927, DOI https://doi.org/10.5535/arm.2011.35.6.922, OA PDF above. Single case report/review; no randomized intervention. It describes lipo-lymphedema and warns that excessive diuretic use can produce pseudo-Bartter syndrome (hyperaldosteronism/hypokalemia). Not evidence of efficacy.
- PMID 15006533, **Painful fat syndrome in a male patient**, Br J Plast Surg 2004, DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bjps.2003.12.020. Single male case; abstract says weight reduction, compression stockings, and diuretic therapy were ineffective. No open full text was located; this is not generalizable.
- PMID 28913000, **Lipolymphedema Associated with Idiopathic Cyclic Edema: A Therapeutic Approach**, 2017, DOI https://doi.org/10.1155/2017/5470909, PMCID PMC5587928. One 55-year-old woman with lipedema plus lymphedema/idiopathic cyclic edema; prior diuretics were reportedly ineffective; calcium dobesilate was used without conventional-diuretic comparator. Case report.
- Pereira de Godoy & Guerreiro Godoy, **Hypotheses and Evolution in the Current Treatment of Lipedema Syndrome**, J Clin Med Res 2022;14:106-107, DOI https://doi.org/10.14740/jocmr4666. Letter/editorial; hypothesizes ~10% idiopathic cyclic edema and mentions calcium dobesilate, aminaphtone, and Ginkgo biloba. No trial.
- PMID 15006533 and PMID 36970766 (Sodium in the skin, 2023; DOI https://doi.org/10.1093/ced/llad080) are the only broad PubMed “diuretic” co-occurrences found; the latter is a scoping review, not lipedema treatment evidence. No direct evidence was found for OTC/herbal diuretics.

## C. Adjacent systemic-volume/edema evidence

- Acute decompensated heart failure: Michigan Medicine, **Inpatient Diuretic Guideline for Patients with Acute Decompensated Heart Failure**, NCBI Bookshelf NBK589894 (2022), https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK589894/?report=reader . Loop diuretics relieve congestion/symptoms but have not shown direct mortality benefit. The DOSE randomized trial (Felker et al., PMID 21366472, DOI https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1005419; 308 patients) found no bolus-vs-infusion difference in symptoms/creatinine; high-dose produced more fluid/weight loss but more transient worsening renal function (24% vs 14%, p=.04). The guideline recommends reassessing volume status and monitoring weight, intake/output, vital signs, renal function and electrolytes; dosing is intentionally omitted here.
- HF disease modification is distinct from conventional diuresis. DAPA-HF, PMID 31535829, DOI https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1911303 (4744 HFrEF patients) reduced worsening HF/CV death with dapagliflozin; EMPEROR-Reduced, PMID 32865377, DOI https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2022190 (3730 HFrEF) reduced the composite and slowed eGFR decline; EMPEROR-Preserved, PMID 34449189, DOI https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2107038 (5988 EF>40%) reduced the composite, mainly HF hospitalization; DELIVER, PMID 36027570, DOI https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2206286, extended outcome evidence across mildly reduced/preserved EF. The open PRESERVED-HF RCT (NCT03030235; DOI https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-021-01536-x; Nature Medicine 2021) randomized 324 HFpEF patients, 88% on loop diuretics, for 12 weeks: dapagliflozin improved KCCQ and 6-minute walk distance; volume depletion was 6.8% vs 4.3%, AKI 3.1% in each arm. It was funded by AstraZeneca with disclosed author relationships and was not a lipedema study. SGLT2 inhibitors are therefore disease-modifying in selected HF/diabetes/renal indications, not lipedema treatments and not interchangeable with loop/thiazide drugs.
- Cirrhosis/ascites: Aithal et al., **Guidelines on the management of ascites in cirrhosis**, Gut 2021, DOI https://doi.org/10.1136/gutjnl-2020-321790, PMID 33067334, PMCID PMC7788190, OA. GRADE-based guideline: spironolactone for a first moderate presentation; spironolactone plus furosemide for recurrent/severe ascites or faster diuresis; monitor adverse events; stop diuretics and expand plasma volume for hypovolemic hyponatremia. It cites randomized regimens (Fogel 1981; Santos 2003; Angeli 2010) but evidence is disease-specific and not lipedema.
- Nephrotic edema: Nishi et al., **Evidence-based clinical practice guidelines for nephrotic syndrome 2014**, Clin Exp Nephrol 2016, DOI https://doi.org/10.1007/s10157-015-1216-x, PMID 27099136, PMCID PMC4891386, OA. Japanese evidence-based guideline: oral loop alone or loop plus thiazide can reduce edema; IV loop for severe edema; evidence on albumin is uncertain and it may worsen hypertension. Older, disease-specific guidance; KDIGO 2021 full text was not obtained.
- Hypertension: ALLHAT (The ALLHAT Officers and Coordinators, PMID 12479763, DOI https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.288.23.2981; 33,357 high-risk hypertensive adults) found no difference in fatal CHD/nonfatal MI for chlorthalidone vs amlodipine/lisinopril, but less HF with chlorthalidone; this supports use for hypertension indication, not lipedema. OPTiMISE exploratory analysis (PMID 33959004, DOI https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2021.619088; 569 adults >=80) found class-specific BP changes after withdrawing one antihypertensive, but was short, post-hoc, and not diuretic-specific. SPRINT post-hoc analyses cannot prove diuretic causality.
- Medication-induced leg edema: Ely et al., **Approach to leg edema of unclear etiology**, J Am Board Fam Med 2006, DOI https://doi.org/10.3122/jabfm.19.2.148, PMID 16513903, OA PDF. Older clinical review, not a formal guideline; discusses idiopathic/cyclic edema, venous disease, basic renal/thyroid/albumin workup, and older practice patterns such as spironolactone. This is contradictory/low-level context and should not override newer lipedema/ISL guidance. Calcium-channel-blocker edema is a capillary hydrostatic-pressure redistribution phenomenon rather than proof of total-body volume overload; evaluate the culprit medication and systemic/venous causes rather than assuming a diuretic will fix it.

## D. Withdrawal/rebound and monitoring

Van Poelgeest et al., **The effects of diuretic deprescribing in adult patients: A systematic review to inform an evidence-based diuretic deprescribing guideline**, Br J Clin Pharmacol 2025;91:38-54, online 08.08.2024, DOI https://doi.org/10.1111/bcp.16189, PMID 39117602, PMCID PMC11671325, OA. PROSPERO CRD42022371658; Medline/Embase/Cochrane inception-16.12.2022, updated 28.05.2024; 7449 records, 78 full texts, 25 reports/22 studies (15 RCT, 7 nonrandomized), no meta-analysis because of heterogeneity. None was lipedema-specific. Ten studies were HF, six hypertension, two peripheral edema; follow-up 2 weeks-6 years. Evidence was low/very low; 10/18 randomized studies had high risk of bias. HF recurrence after withdrawal ranged 10-58% inconsistently; ankle edema was reported in 6% and 11% in low-certainty trials; failures generally reflected recurrence of HF/HTN. The review supports selected, monitored deprescribing, not a universal pharmacologic-rebound claim. Funding/COI: EU NETPHARM support for one author; another reported grants/salary award/honorarium; others declared none or no relevant conflicts.

Mechanism map for “rebound edema”:
1. Compression non-adherence: the US SOC uses “rebound edema” for non-compliance before surgery, not diuretic withdrawal.
2. Post-diuretic sodium retention: short-acting loop effect wanes after several hours and kidneys can temporarily reverse sodium losses; this is pharmacodynamic, not proof of disease relapse.
3. Underlying HF, renal/nephrotic, cirrhotic, or hypertensive disease: withdrawal may expose recurrence of the original disease; this is the dominant explanation in the deprescribing review.
4. Rare chronic diuretic dependence/idiopathic edema: two case patients in Kuchel & Ethier 1998, DOI https://doi.org/10.1159/000013371, PMID 9730576, were described with severe cardiorespiratory failure after withdrawal and proposed secondary hyperaldosteronism/ANF suppression. Case-level evidence, no lipedema, no open full text.
5. Excess diuresis: volume depletion, hypotension, AKI, and sodium/potassium/magnesium disturbances are adverse effects of treatment, not rebound.

General article-level monitoring principle: any diuretic used for a documented systemic indication requires clinician review of diagnosis, blood pressure/orthostasis, weight and symptoms, renal function/eGFR, sodium/potassium/magnesium/bicarbonate, and indication-specific urine protein, albumin, liver or cardiac markers. Urgent assessment is warranted for new dyspnea/orthopnea, chest pain/syncope/confusion, oliguria, rapid generalized edema/ascites, severe weakness/palpitations, hypotension, or a unilateral painful/red swollen leg. No taper or dose protocol is supplied.

## Material gaps and tool families

Used: Europe PMC and PMC search/full-text downloads; NCBI Bookshelf search/download; Crossref DOI/search; PubMed E-utilities raw XML/JSON; ClinicalTrials.gov search and study-detail endpoints; OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar/Google Scholar discovery; PDF-to-text downloads; shell/curl/grep for inspectable extraction; artifact list/read/grep/register/delete. Retraction Watch was queried with DOI terms but returned broad unrelated records, so those artifacts were deleted and no direct retraction/update was inferred. Chemistry, patent, and video tools were not relevant.

Gaps: no direct lipedema diuretic RCT or registered drug trial; no direct evidence for OTC/herbal diuretics, acetazolamide, MRAs, potassium-sparing agents, thiazides, loops, SGLT2 inhibitors, or lipedema-specific deprescribing; no separate 2024/2025 systematic review dedicated solely to rebound/withdrawal/monitoring; several landmark trials remain paywalled at full-text level (DAPA-HF, EMPEROR trials, DELIVER, ALLHAT); Kuchel 1998 and the 2004 male case had no open full text; KDIGO 2021 full text was not retrieved.

### Added medication-induced edema evidence
- Makani et al., **Peripheral edema associated with calcium channel blockers: incidence and withdrawal rate—a meta-analysis of randomized trials**, J Hypertens 2011;29:1270-1280, PMID 21558959, DOI https://doi.org/10.1097/HJH.0b013e3283472643, PubMed publication month July 2011. Systematic search of PubMed/EMBASE/CENTRAL (1980-Jan 2011), 106 RCT reports/99,469 participants: edema 10.7% with CCB vs 3.2% controls and withdrawal for edema 2.1% vs 0.5%; higher with dihydropyridines and dose. Abstract/full indexed record is preserved; publisher full text was not open in this run. The mechanism is precapillary hydrostatic pressure/redistribution, so this is not evidence that total-body volume overload should be treated by adding a diuretic.
